We looked, and found two ways of making it ── which, in wave language, turned out to be the same thing.

99 % of the proton's mass is not quark mass. What is it, then ── the energy of a confined field.

★ In wave language ──

A wave confined in a box has a lowest frequency.
That lowest frequency appears as mass.

Episode 6's "mass = cutoff frequency" doubles as the explanation of where mass comes from.

02Put it in a box and mass appears

Boundary conditions cut off the wavenumber from below $$\text{1D box}:\ k=\frac{\pi}{L}\ \Rightarrow\ E=\frac{\pi\hbar c}{L},\qquad \text{spherical box (lowest)}:\ kR=2.04\ \Rightarrow\ E=\frac{2.04\,\hbar c}{R}$$

\(\hbar c=197.327\) MeV·fm

Confine anything to 1 fm and you get hundreds of MeV ── the same order as the proton's 938.

Invert it ── if three massless quarks carried the whole mass $$3\times\frac{2.04\,\hbar c}{R}=938.3\ \text{MeV}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R=1.287\ \text{fm}$$

The measured charge radius is 0.841 fm ── off by a factor of 1.53

Being honest about it

A factor of 1.5. The order of magnitude is right, and that is all.
★ This is a sanity check on "confinement produces mass", not a calculation of the proton mass. The real thing is lattice QCD, which now reproduces it to 1 %.
What we did was see that "why hundreds of MeV?" is set by \(\hbar c/\)(1 fm).

§04 ── "constants are integrals" holds for mass too

\(m_b(m_Z)=3.107\) GeV (published about 2.83 GeV, with two loops and beyond). One loop is 9 % off, but the run from 4.18 to 3.1 is there.

★ A caveat ── \(m_b(\mu)\) is dimensionful. By Episode 7's rule it should be bookkeeping. What is observable is a ratio.

So we looked at \(m_b/m_\tau\) (the tau does not run under QCD, so the ratio moves).
★ At \(\mu=2.8\times10^7\) GeV the b quark and the tau have the same mass, and at the grand-unification scale \(10^{16}\) GeV, \(m_b/m_\tau=0.68\) ── close to 1, but not equal.

This is a known result (b–τ unification fails in the Standard Model and improves with supersymmetry, as the standard argument goes). We merely reproduced it at one loop.

§05 ── the equivalence principle, said as a wave

Gravity is "clocks run at different rates in different places" (Episode 16's \(c_{\rm eff}\)).
And a dispersion relation is written against that clock.

★ So every wave is affected identically, whatever it is made of.
That is "gravitational mass = inertial mass", in wave language.

Conversely ── the equivalence principle can only fail if different kinds of wave see different clocks. Which is why one searches for a fifth force.

Episode 6 said mass is a cutoff frequency. What this episode adds is ── ★ there are two ways to make that cutoff.
Put it in a box, or soak it in a medium.

Episode 17's superconductor was case ② (a condensate giving the photon an intercept); Episode 16's waveguide was case ① (boundaries giving the photon an intercept).
★ The photon in a waveguide, the photon in a superconductor, the proton and the electron all acquire mass by the same mechanism.

Exercises

  1. Answer in one sentence: what is 99 % of your body weight?
    Show answer
    Gluon fields and kinetic energy confined inside protons and neutrons.
    Almost all your weight is nucleon mass, and 99 % of nucleon mass is confinement energy ── the Higgs contribution (the bare quark masses) is only about 1 %.
    ★ One often hears "the Higgs gives things mass"; for the mass around you, that is a statement about 1 %.
  2. What do a photon in a waveguide, a photon in a superconductor and a proton have in common?
    Show answer
    All three have an intercept in their dispersion relation.
    Waveguide: boundary conditions give \(\omega_c=\pi c/a\) (Episode 6)
    Superconductor: a condensate gives \(\omega_c=c/\lambda_L\) (Episode 17; 5.06 eV in niobium)
    Proton: confinement gives \(\omega_c\sim2.04c/R\) (this episode)
    ★ The only difference is boundary conditions versus background field; what comes out is the same "mass".
  3. Did Episode 3's hypothesis "mass is an integral" come out right for mass?
    Show answer
    Half right ── but the integration variable is \(\ln\mu\), not time.
    \(\ln m(\mu)=\ln m(\mu_0)-\int\gamma(\alpha_s)\,d\ln\mu\) is literally an integral (§04).
    ★ With Episode 7's proviso: \(m(\mu)\) is dimensionful, hence bookkeeping, and what is observable is a ratio like \(m_b/m_\tau\).
    Exactly the structure of Episode 5's couplings: the running is real, but only the dimensionless part means anything.

Summary　There are only two ways to make an intercept

Of the proton's 938.27 MeV, the quark masses sum to 8.99 MeV ── 0.96 %. The other 99 % is the energy of a confined field. So is 99 % of your body weight.

In wave language it is simple ── a wave in a box has a lowest frequency. \(\pi\hbar c/L\) is 620 MeV at 1 fm, the same order as the proton. Inverting gives \(R=1.29\) fm against a measured 0.841 ── a factor of 1.5, which is the crudeness of the model.

The other source is coupling to a condensate. Inverting the Yukawa couplings, only \(y_{\rm top}=0.9919\) is of order one, with five and a half orders down to the electron ── an unexplained hierarchy.

And mass runs (\(m_b:4.18\to3.11\) GeV at one loop). At \(\mu=2.8\times10^7\) GeV the b and the tau coincide, and at \(10^{16}\) GeV \(m_b/m_\tau=0.68\). "Constants are integrals" holds for mass, with \(\ln\mu\) as the variable.

★ In wave language, confinement and condensate are the same thing ──
both create an intercept in the dispersion relation, one by boundaries, one by a background field.

The photon in a waveguide (Episode 6), the photon in a superconductor (Episode 17), the proton and the electron ── all four acquire mass by the same mechanism.

The Yukawa couplings are inverted from the tree-level relation \(m=yv/\sqrt2\), ignoring scale dependence (including for \(y_{\rm top}\)). The running mass is one loop only and comes out 9 % above the published \(m_b(m_Z)\simeq2.83\) GeV ── two loops and proper quark thresholds improve it.
