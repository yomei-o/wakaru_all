★★★ The divergent term’s coefficient \(A\) ranges from 0.443 to 1.832. The constant is \(-0.083333\) every time (largest departure from \(-1/12\): 6.7e−6).
★★ Episode 45’s footnote can now be closed.
★ And a phrasing worth correcting: \(-1/12\) is not “the value of” \(1+2+3+\cdots\). That series diverges. \(-1/12\) is what remains after the divergent term is subtracted. ★★★ The point is that the term you must subtract depends on the cutoff, and the remainder does not.

Exercises

  1. §02 has four routes landing on the same \(6\alpha\). Is that deep?
    Show answer
    No ── all four reduce to one Fourier transform.
    ★ Differentiation multiplies by \(i\omega\), fractional differentiation by \((i\omega)^\alpha\), and both a window’s sidelobes and a detrending gain are \(\omega^\alpha\) behaviour as \(\omega\to\infty\) or \(\omega\to0\). Every one is “multiply by a power in Fourier space.”
    ★★ Laying them side by side is still worth doing: only then can you see they are the same, and carry a result from one to another. ★ Episode 60’s \(\beta\le2q+3\) was obtained exactly that way ── by carrying Episode 58’s window argument over to detrending.
    ★★★ In Episode 55’s terms, an analogy is not a claim. “They all land on \(6\alpha\)” is a fact, but it is not new physics ── it is tidying the tools, and should be written as such.
  2. §03 checks that \(-1/12\) is regulator-independent. Why is it?
    Show answer
    Under a Mellin transform, \(-1/12\) emerges as a residue of \(\zeta(-1)\).
    ★ Writing \(\sum n f(\delta n)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\delta^{-s}\zeta(s-1)F(s)\,ds\) (with \(F\) the Mellin transform of \(f\)), the pole at \(s=2\) gives \(A/\delta^2\) and the pole at \(s=0\) gives the constant.
    ★★ The residue at \(s=0\) is \(\zeta(-1)f(0)\) ── as long as \(f(0)=1\), the shape of \(f\) is irrelevant. ★ What does depend on \(f\) is \(A=F(2)\), which is the 0.443–1.832 in the table.
    ★★★ What this episode did numerically is confirm that analysis ── five cutoffs were tried, which is not the same as showing it for all of them. What was shown is “the five we tried agreed.”
  3. §01 wrote too many digits again, despite the handbook. Why did the handbook not prevent it?
    Show answer
    Because step ⑩ says “count the assumptions,” not “vary the fitting range and see.”
    ★ Episode 56’s intercept is not conditional on a model ── it is purely computed. So it passes “count the assumptions” untouched.
    ★★ What caught it was the procedure built in Episode 59 ── vary a condition and see whether the number moves. Here we varied the range of \(T\) and found ±0.04.
    ★★★ So what should be added to ⑩ is “vary the conditions and measure how far it moves.” Episode 59 built that procedure to measure \(\beta\), but it works on any number.
    ★ §01 says writing it in the handbook does not stop you doing it. Writing is not enough; you only find out by varying ── that is this episode’s practical lesson.

Summary — closing Part XIX

★ The nine numbers of Episodes 56–60 were recomputed and all reproduce. But Episode 56’s intercept −2.1708 had too many digits: it moves by ±0.04 with the range of \(T\), so “about −2.2” is the right statement. One episode after Episode 55 issued that very warning, we did it again.

★★★ \(\text{dB/oct}=6\alpha\) returned the same value along four independent routes ──
differentiation, fractional differentiation, a window’s edge, and subtracting a trend.

★ And \(\alpha\) need not be an integer (order 0.5 gives 3.010 against a predicted 3.010). ★★ But all four share one Fourier transform at the root, so this is not new physics ── it is tidying the tools (Exercise 1).

★★★ And Episode 45’s open item is closed ── \(-1/12\) comes out the same for five cutoffs (largest departure 6.7e−6), while the divergent coefficient \(A\) ranges over 0.443–1.832. ★ \(-1/12\) is not “the value of” \(1+2+3+\cdots\); it is what remains once the divergent term is subtracted.

★★ That is where Episode 60’s running variance parts company ── the Casimir case leaves a cutoff-independent remainder and the \(1/f^\beta\) variance does not, so one determines a physical quantity and the other only forces you to state the record length. Whether a running quantity can be given meaning depends on whether such a remainder survives.

★ The handbook is now 14 steps. ⑪ subtract a trend, ⑫ check the two conditions separately, ⑬ measure the error’s shape first, ⑭ give a variance its record length ── all of them declarations of where you cut.

★★★ The biggest item is unchanged: this series has still never touched real measured data.
