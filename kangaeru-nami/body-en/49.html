A Handbook —
What to do when you are handed a measurement Measuring the slope is not enough. Two things must always be looked at with it — how many decades it lasts, and what the phase does.
★ The phase can be predicted from the amplitude slope alone (the Bode gain–phase relation; numerically within 0.01°) — and any departure means an extra delay.
★★ The centrepiece: we catch data that "looks like 1/f but isn't," using nothing but the decade count.

Exercises

  1. In §03 the machine picked D's flat low-frequency region. Is that a flaw in the procedure?
    Show answer
    Not a flaw — the question was underspecified. \(e^{-\omega}\) really is constant at low frequency, and "order 0 over 3 decades" is a correct measurement.
    ★ The procedure answers "where is it flat," not "where is it interesting." What makes a region interesting is your hypothesis.
    ★★ Which is why §04 re-measured with a specified target: "how many decades at \(-0.5\)?" — a procedure only becomes sharp once there is a hypothesis. ★ Episode 15's rule about declaring falsifiability pays off here too.
  2. §06 read a delay off the phase. What if the phase departs but there is no extra delay?
    Show answer
    Then the system is not minimum-phase. The Bode relation holds only when there are no right-half-plane zeros.
    ★ A right-half-plane zero rotates the phase further while leaving the amplitude alone — an all-pass component is present.
    ★★ A delay is in fact the limiting case of all-pass (\(e^{-i\omega T}\) has unit amplitude and rotates only phase) — so §06's delay was a special case of this general rule.
    ★ To tell them apart, check whether the departure is proportional to \(\omega\) — proportional means a pure delay; otherwise a right-half-plane zero.
  3. §07 says not to test a theory with an insensitive quantity. How do you actually carry that out?
    Show answer
    Compute, in advance, how much that quantity moves if the theory changes.
    ★ Episode 42 found that intermittency moves the dB/oct by only 0.081 — with a measurement error of 0.1 dB/oct, that measurement can never distinguish them.
    ★★ Episode 47 found \(\phi^2\) passing on \(n_s\) but excluded by \(r\) — \(r\) is the sensitive one.
    ★ So: compute "the difference you want to test" ÷ "your measurement error" before measuring. If it is below 1, the measurement is a waste. ★ This is the most practical habit the series acquired in 49 episodes.

Summary — the slope alone is not enough

Forty-eight episodes of procedure, gathered into seven steps — ① plot log–log ② measure the local order ③ count the decades ④ find the bends ⑤ measure the phase ⑥ change the moment ⑦ compare with the system size.

The phase can be predicted from the amplitude slope alone (the Bode gain–phase relation) — numerically matching \(90\alpha\) to within 0.01°. ★ Episode 1's "phase \(=90\alpha\) degrees" came from here.

Run mechanically on six "unknown" datasets: C is a power law across the whole window, B and E have one bend each, D is constant at low frequency. ★ The machine returns the longest plateau, so the region you care about has to be specified by you.

★★★ The centrepiece —
A (a broad superposition) and F (a narrow distribution) both look like 1/f,
but order \(-0.5\) holds for 6.898 decades in A and 0.498 in F.
F merely passes through \(-0.5\).

★ "The slope was \(-0.5\) at one point" is no evidence — every smooth function passes through \(-0.5\) somewhere. What guaranteed Episode 2's "many time constants" was the decade count.

And the phase reveals an extra delay — the amplitude is identical, but the phase drifts in proportion to \(\omega\), and dividing that gap by \(\omega\) gives the delay \(T\). ★ Causality (Episode 8) is also a measuring instrument.

★ Honestly: the handbook narrows candidates; it cannot pin down one mechanism. And everything here was tested on noiseless functions — with real data, the decade counts carry statistical error.

On the numbers here: the numerical check of the Bode gain–phase relation, the automatic diagnosis of six synthetic datasets (longest flat window, decade counts, bend detection), the A-versus-F comparison, and the phase shift from a delay were all computed here. The Bode gain–phase relation, minimum-phase systems and all-pass components, the Kramers–Kronig relations, and the fact that a superposition of many time constants produces 1/f (the Debye superposition of Episode 2) are all well-known results and none are discoveries of this article — what this article did was arrange them into a single procedure, actually run that procedure as a program, and show where it helps and where it falls short. The six datasets in §03–04 are all synthetic and constructed here; none is a real measurement. The criteria used ("variation within 0.1," "decades within \(\pm0.1\)") are conventions adopted here; different criteria give different decade counts. Only noiseless functions were treated; with noisy real data the decade estimates acquire statistical error (outside this article's scope).
