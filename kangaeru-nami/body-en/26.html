Thinking in Waves Episode 26 ／ Part XI · Settling the hypothesis

Grading Episode 2's hypothesis with 25 episodes' worth of tools

Is a fractional order always the result of coarse-graining? ──
we found a counterexample Integer-order parts alone can build a half order. A fractional order always decomposes into first-order relaxations.
And yet ── there is a real system that coarse-grains down to nothing but an integer order.
★ What was missing is self-similarity. A fractional order is the trace of scale-invariant degrees of freedom that were removed.

In Episode 2 we wrote this ── [Hypothesis] A system that shows a fractional order is always the result of coarse-graining (integrating out) hidden degrees of freedom.
And also: "one counterexample would refute it ── this is falsifiable." Twenty-five episodes have supplied the tools, so let us actually check.

02Test ① Building a half order from integer-order parts only

Make a ladder out of resistors and capacitors. Both parts are integer order ── a resistor is \(Z=R\) (order 0), a capacitor \(Z=1/(i\omega C)\) (order \(-1\)). There is no half-order component anywhere.

Input impedance of \(N\) stages ── a continued fraction $$Z_n=R+\cfrac{1}{i\omega C+\cfrac{1}{Z_{n-1}}}$$

The heart of §02

★★ Built from integer-order parts only ── and out comes a half order.

\(-10\) dB/dec \(=-3.01\) dB/oct \(=6\alpha\) gives \(\alpha=-1/2\), and the phase \(-45^\circ=90\alpha\) degrees ── exactly the half-rung of Episode 3's ladder.
This is what the Warburg impedance of Episode 2 really is. Coarse-graining (folding infinitely many stages into one impedance) genuinely can create a fractional order.

03Test ② The other direction ── does a fractional order always decompose?

Now reverse it. Can a fractional-order response be written as a superposition of ordinary first-order relaxations?

Spectral representation ── fractional order = a continuum of first orders? $$(i\omega)^{-\alpha}\ \stackrel{?}{=}\ \int_0^\infty g(\tau)\,\frac{1}{1+i\omega\tau}\,d\tau$$

The \(1/(1+i\omega\tau)\) on the right is a first-order relaxation of time constant \(\tau\) ── plain integer order

The answer is known (for \(0<\alpha<1\)). We check it numerically, slicing \(\tau\) logarithmically and summing.

The shape of the distribution $$g(\tau)=\frac{\sin\alpha\pi}{\pi}\,\tau^{\alpha-1}$$

The heart of §03

★★ Any fractional order with \(0<\alpha<1\) can be written as a continuum of first-order relaxations.
This is effectively a proof of claim ② ── behind any dissipative fractional-order response, a distribution of hidden degrees of freedom can always be assigned.
★ And that distribution is \(g(\tau)\propto\tau^{\alpha-1}\) ── a power law, i.e. scale invariant. This one line is what matters later.

04Test ③ Does coarse-graining always give a fractional order?

★ This is the real question. We go looking for a counterexample. Take the most innocent coarse-graining there is ── a semi-infinite chain of masses and springs, of which we keep only the end mass.

A uniform chain, and the "force from the medium" left at its end $$m\ddot x_n=k(x_{n+1}-2x_n+x_{n-1}),\qquad Z=\frac{F}{v}$$

The heart of §04 ── the counterexample

★★ Phase 0° ── integer order. Not fractional.

Infinitely many degrees of freedom were removed and what came out is a plain friction coefficient \(\sqrt{km}\).
★ "Remove degrees of freedom and you always get a fractional order" is false. Episode 2's hypothesis does not stand as written.

★★ The exponent of the removed degrees of freedom becomes the order itself (\(\alpha=s\)).

Exercises

  1. If each stage of the RC ladder were scaled stage by stage as \(R\to2R,\ C\to C/2\), what happens to the order (qualitatively)?
    Show the answer
    It moves off the half order. The half order here comes from every stage being identical in shape ── the product \(RC\) is the same at every stage (the simplest form of self-similarity).
    Make the time constants \(\tau_n=R_nC_n\) a geometric sequence and the removed degrees of freedom become evenly spaced on a log axis, moving the order to a different value set by the ratio.
    ★ The §05 conclusion ── "the order is set by the distribution of the hidden time constants" ── applies directly.
  2. How would you rebuild the spring chain (Test ③) so that it does give a fractional order?
    Show the answer
    Let the mass or the spring constant vary as a power of position (e.g. \(m_n\propto n^{p}\)). The low-frequency density of states then becomes \(J(\omega)\propto\omega^s\) with \(s\ne1\), and the §05 table gives order \(s\).
    Or make it branch (a fractal chain) ── branching gives a density of states whose power is set by the fractal dimension.
    ★ The uniform chain is integer order because it is uniform, not because it was coarse-grained.
  3. Was Episode 2's hypothesis "wrong"? What is the scientifically accurate thing to say?
    Show the answer
    One direction was right, the other wrong. "Fractional order → a distribution of hidden degrees of freedom can be written" is effectively proved by Test ②. "Coarse-graining → always fractional" is refuted by the counterexample in Test ③.
    ★ And being refuted let us rewrite the hypothesis into a stronger form ── "when the removed degrees of freedom are distributed scale-invariantly."
    ★ On Episode 15's scoreboard this would read "◎ holds, conditionally". A hypothesis declared falsifiable was actually falsified, and got sharper for it ── that is this episode's result.

Summary　The hypothesis was half right, and got rewritten

We split Episode 2's hypothesis ── "a system that shows a fractional order is always the result of coarse-graining hidden degrees of freedom" ── into three and graded each.

Test ①: stack 1000 stages of a ladder made only of resistors and capacitors (both integer order) and the phase is \(-44.80^\circ\) with a slope of \(-9.98\) dB/dec ── a half order. It matches the exact semi-infinite solution \(Z_\infty=R/2+\sqrt{R^2/4+R/(i\omega C)}\). Integer-order parts alone can make a fractional order.

Test ②: any fractional order with \(0<\alpha<1\) can be written as a continuum of first-order relaxations (\(g(\tau)=\sin(\alpha\pi)/\pi\cdot\tau^{\alpha-1}\), relative error \(10^{-3}\)–\(10^{-6}\)) ── see a fractional order and a distribution of hidden degrees of freedom can always be assigned.

Test ③ ── ★ the counterexample: keep only the end mass of a uniform semi-infinite mass–spring chain and the impedance is a pure resistance \(\sqrt{km}\) at phase 0°. Infinitely many degrees of freedom removed, and still integer order. "Coarse-graining → always fractional" is false.

The difference was how the removed degrees of freedom were distributed. With a bath of spectral density \(J(\omega)\propto\omega^s\) the order is exactly \(s\) (slope \(s\), phase \(90s\) degrees, confirmed numerically). A uniform chain is Ohmic (\(s=1\)) ── which is why it was integer order.

★ Rewritten hypothesis: a fractional order appears when the coarse-grained
degrees of freedom are distributed scale-invariantly (as a power law).

Together with Episode 13 (imaginary order = scale transformation) and Episode 22 (wavelets = log axis) ── all three turned on scale invariance. A fractional order is the trace of scale-invariant degrees of freedom that were removed. One of Episode 15's open doors closes, conditionally; only the origin of 1/f stays open, and in a changed form ── "why are log-uniform distributions of time constants so common in nature?"
