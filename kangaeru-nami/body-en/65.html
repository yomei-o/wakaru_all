The core of §02

★★★ Slicing flattens \(\beta\) by 1 (3.01 dB/oct) per dimension removed. Cutting a 3D field down to 1D flattens it by 6 dB/oct — which is, I think, where the “a dimension is 6 dB” intuition comes from. But that is two dimensions.
★★★ Projection does not change \(\beta\) at all. In Fourier space it just takes the \(k_\perp=0\) section, so the exponent is preserved.

The core of §03 ── the first summit here

★★★ “The spectral shape is unchanged” does not mean “no information was lost”. Projection preserves \(\beta\) while discarding every \(k_\perp\ne0\) component.
★★ This is precisely the theme of Episodes 59 and 60 ── an apparent quantity can be preserved while something is still being lost.
★ Which is why CT works: collect enough directions and \(k_\perp\) fills back in. The number needed is about \(\pi N/2\) (sampling).
★★ And the region near the origin fills first while the outside stays sparse ── that unevenness is why CT resolution is good at the centre and poor at the edge.

The core of §04

★★★ The 3D dimension matches theory exactly (1.4650–2.8928, box counting to four figures).
★★★ But the projection never exceeds 2, however large \(D\) becomes.
★★ “A non-integer dimension introduces error” is wrong: whatever exceeds 2 disappears entirely. Photograph an object of \(D=2.89\) and the image carries two dimensions’ worth. The remaining 0.89 does not show up as error — it looks as though it was never there.

05★★ Symmetry axes are the exceptional directions ── the worst view

Marstrand says “for almost every direction”. Outside that “almost” lie the exceptional directions ── and a lattice’s symmetry axes are exactly those.

The axis-projection dimension is set by \(\log(\)number of sub-cells that survive without collapsing\()/\log3\) — a combinatorial quantity, not Marstrand’s value.

★ Deliberately avoiding low-index orientations when imaging crystals is exactly this.

06★★★ Is mass a non-integer-dimensional wave? ── Episode 2’s integral

A single mass = a single pole (a Lorentzian). A non-integer scaling dimension = a power-law continuum of masses.

Exercises

  1. §03 says projection preserves \(\beta\) yet loses information. So if you only want \(\beta\), is one projection enough?
    Show answer
    Yes ── and that is the point.
    ★ If \(\beta\) is all you want, one projection gives the right value. ★★ The danger is concluding “the \(\beta\) is right, so reconstruction must be possible too”.
    ★★★ Episode 59 built a test on “if the value moves when you change the window, the window is to blame”. Here it is the converse ── the value does not move and information is still lost. Not moving is evidence neither of correctness nor of sufficiency.
    ★ For the handbook: count “what is preserved” and “what is needed to reconstruct” separately. \(\beta\) is the former, \(k_\perp\) the latter.
  2. §04 says whatever exceeds 2 vanishes. Then how would you ever learn that the object has \(D>2\)?
    Show answer
    Not from one view. You need several.
    ★ Every direction gives \(\min(D,2)=2\). A single image tells you “\(D\ge2\)” and cannot distinguish 2.1 from 2.9.
    ★★ §03’s counting is the answer: collect \(\pi N/2\) views, reconstruct in 3D, then box-count in 3D.
    ★★★ So “measuring a non-integer dimension” is itself a tomography problem. CT is needed not only to see shape but to measure dimension.
    ★ Conversely: a fractal dimension estimated from a single photograph and coming out near 2 might be genuinely 2, or might be anything above it. A value pinned at 2 should be suspected of saturation.
  3. Is \(d_U=2-\alpha\) in §06 a discovery?
    Show answer
    No — it is the same integral rewritten. Episode 61 said that four routes landing on \(6\alpha\) is tidying the tools, not new physics, because they share one Fourier transform at the root. This is the same kind of thing.
    ★ Episode 2’s superposition integral and the unparticle spectral representation are one integral with different variable names. ★★ Laying them side by side is still worth doing: it shows that “non-integer dimension” names the same thing as \(1/f\)’s “many time constants”.
    ★★★ And it makes the boundary sharp: the correspondence says anything you like about kinematics (what the spectrum looks like) and nothing at all about dynamics (why that \(\alpha\), why that mass). ★ In Episode 55’s terms this is a translation — neither a measurement nor a value conditional on a model.

Summary — four questions, all four counted

① “Is a dimension a 6 dB filter?” ── Close; a dimension is 3 dB. One integration is \(\alpha\) +1 = 6.02 dB/oct; one sliced dimension is \(\beta\) −1 = 3.01 dB/oct. Exactly a factor of two apart. ★ Cutting 3D down to 1D flattens by 6 dB/oct, which is probably where the intuition came from — but that is two dimensions.

★★★ ② “Observing loses information” ── yes, and there are two kinds.

★ Slicing changes the shape (\(\beta_1=\beta_d-(d-1)\), verified numerically). ★★★ Projection changes nothing about the shape and still loses the information ── one view of an \(N^3\) object keeps \(1/N\); filling \(k\)-space takes about \(\pi N/2\) views (201 at \(N=128\), and the measured coverage reaches ~100 % at exactly 201). “Same spectral shape” does not mean “nothing lost”.

★★★ ③ “Non-integer dimension gives error” ── no, it saturates at 2. Marstrand’s projection theorem. The 3D dimension matches theory exactly (1.4650–2.8928) while the projection never exceeds 2. ★★ What exceeds 2 does not appear as error; it disappears.

★★ A bonus: symmetry axes are the theorem’s exceptional directions. The axis-projection dimension is a combinatorial quantity (“sub-cells that survive collapse”), lower than \(\min(D,2)\) — 1.686 on axis versus 1.871 generic at \(m=16\). The easiest direction to look is the most lossy.

★★★ ④ “Could mass be a non-integer-dimensional wave?” ── that integral was already in Episode 2. A single mass is a single pole; a non-integer dimension is a power-law continuum of masses. Episode 2’s superposition integral and the unparticle spectral representation are identical, giving \(d_U=2-\alpha\), with \(1/f\) (\(\alpha=1\)) landing exactly on \(d_U=1\) — one pole, an ordinary particle.

★ And in AdS/CFT it holds literally (\(\Delta(\Delta-d)=m^2L^2\), Episode 39) ── the third appearance of Episode 13’s quadratic. ★★ Honestly: this is a translation, not an explanation of mass. It speaks to kinematics and is silent on dynamics (Exercise 3).

The §02 numbers average 24 realizations in 2D and 3 in 3D; the projection samples are few and ±0.1 scatter remains.
