Thinking in Waves Episode 54 / Part XVIII — Putting Error Bars On It

Is there a way around "range goes as \(\log N\)"?

Can Range Be Bought? —
Not with time, but with instruments Episode 53 concluded that range grows as \(\log N\). ★ So how does one get more of it?
★ There are three ways — splice bands, use a different observable, change the system itself.
★★ And the price came out too — splicing costs "1 dB of calibration error across a 3-decade gap = 0.1 dB/oct." Exactly the size of Episode 42's target (0.081).

Analytic estimate (treating each band as a point) $$\text{slope error [dB/oct]}\approx\frac{0.3\times\delta\,[\mathrm{dB}]}{\text{separation in decades}}$$

The point of §02

At \(S=3,\ \delta=1\) dB, measured 0.0964 against the predicted 0.1000 — the bands' own width makes the measurement slightly smaller.
★★★ 1 dB of calibration error across a 3-decade gap costs 0.1 dB/oct. The same size as Episode 42's 0.081.
★★ So buying 3 decades by splicing requires calibration better than 0.8 dB.
★ Read the other way — the further apart the bands, the looser the calibration requirement (6 decades apart, 1 dB costs only 0.05). A conclusion that looks backwards at first: distant instruments are easier to join.

03★★ A different observable — find where the corrections cancel

Assumption: every \(S_p\) is a power of one common \(\rho\) $$S_p(r)=\rho(r)^{\zeta_p},\qquad \rho(r)=r\,h(r)\ (h\ \text{the end correction})$$

In that form \(\ln S_p\) against \(\ln r\) bends, but \(\ln S_p\) against \(\ln S_3\) is a straight line of slope \(\zeta_p/\zeta_3\), whatever \(h\) is.

The point of §03

★★★ Put \(\ln S_3\) on the horizontal axis and the correction \(h(r)\) cancels completely — 4 decades become 9.
★★ This is the idea behind extended self-similarity (ESS), used in experiments.
★★★ But the cancellation happens because we assumed "every \(S_p\) is a power of a common \(\rho\)" — which is not itself proved.
★ What this article showed is only that "if that assumption holds, range can be bought." Per Episode 43, an assumption is written as an assumption.

★★ Gaining one decade of inertial range requires \(Re\) to rise by \(10^{4/3}=21.5\).
★★★ A logarithm again — but this time changing the subject moves the decades.
★ From the laboratory (\(Re=10^4\), 3 decades) to the atmosphere (\(Re=10^{12}\), 9) is a span of 6 — and Episode 51's table gave 0.18 dB/oct at 3 decades and 0.059 at 6.

Exercises

  1. §02 found that bands further apart are easier to calibrate. That seems backwards — why is it true?
    Show answer
    Because a slope is "vertical offset ÷ horizontal distance." The same offset \(\delta\) matters less the further apart the bands are.
    ★ 1 dB costs 0.22 dB/oct across one decade and only 0.05 across six.
    ★★ But separating them leaves a gap with nothing in it — and whether the same power law really continues across that gap has to be established separately.
    ★ So the further apart, the lighter the calibration and the heavier the extrapolation assumption — a trade, as always.
  2. Does §03's ESS really "buy range"?
    Show answer
    Conditionally, yes. What was bought was paid for with the assumption that every \(S_p\) is a power of one common \(\rho\).
    ★ If that holds, \(h(r)\) cancels exactly and the whole span becomes usable (4 decades → 9 in our numbers).
    ★★ If it does not, the cancellation is incomplete — and, worse, the plot still looks straight, which is the dangerous part.
    ★★★ In this series' stance, a \(\zeta_p\) obtained via ESS must be labelled "conditional on an assumption." The same discipline as Episode 43's "do not call organizing a discovery" — do not call an assumption a measurement.
  3. §04 says raising \(Re\) widens the inertial range. Can \(Re\) be raised without limit?
    Show answer
    In the laboratory it very nearly saturates. \(Re=UL/\nu\), so there are three handles — speed \(U\), size \(L\), kinematic viscosity \(\nu\).
    ★ \(U\) is capped by the speed of sound (compressibility changes the physics), \(L\) by the size of the building, \(\nu\) by the choice of fluid (cryogenic helium buys a few decades).
    ★★ So \(Re\approx10^7\) is the practical laboratory ceiling, and beyond that one "uses" the atmosphere or the ocean (\(Re=10^{12}\)).
    ★★★ But using nature costs control — neither isotropy nor stationarity is guaranteed. ★ Episode 22's "stationarity assumption" comes straight back.

Summary — not with time, but with instruments

Episode 53 concluded that range goes as \(\log N\). Here we counted three ways around it — splice, use another observable, change the system.

★ The price of splicing: slope error \(\approx0.3\delta[\mathrm{dB}]/\)(separation in decades). 1 dB across a 3-decade gap costs 0.1 dB/oct — the same size as Episode 42's 0.081. Buying 3 decades needs calibration better than 0.8 dB.

★★ Another observable: if \(S_p=\rho^{\zeta_p}\), then putting \(\ln S_3\) on the horizontal axis makes the correction cancel exactly, turning 4 decades into 9. This is the ESS used in experiments — but the cancellation comes from an assumption, and the assumption is not proved.

★ Changing the system: inertial decades \(=\frac34\log_{10}Re\). 3 in the laboratory (\(10^4\)), 9 in the atmosphere (\(10^{12}\)); one more decade needs \(Re\times21.5\).

★★★ The core — all three buy range, but differently.
Splicing is linear in the number of instruments,
another observable buys it all at once for an assumption,
changing the system buys it as the logarithm of \(Re\).

★★ Episode 53's "range goes as \(\log N\)" was about the same system, the same instrument, measured for longer. Add instruments and range is bought linearly.
★ Honestly: synthetic data only here too. ★ And all three have a price — calibration, an assumption, the size of the apparatus. There was no free range after all.

On the numbers here: the slope error from splicing, the comparison of flat ranges under ESS, and the inertial-range decades against Reynolds number were all computed here. Extended self-similarity, \(L/\eta=Re^{3/4}\), and the general effect of a calibration error on a fitted slope are all well-known results and none are discoveries of this article — what this article did was place them under the single question "how is range bought," and price them in this series' unit of dB/oct. §03's ESS is computed under the assumption that all \(S_p\) are powers of one common \(\rho\); the validity of that assumption is not tested here, and by this series' standard a \(\zeta_p\) obtained via ESS is "conditional on an assumption." The analytic estimate \(0.3\delta/\)separation in §02 treats each band as a point; with bands of finite width the measured value is smaller (0.0964 against 0.1000 at \(S=3\)). The \(Re\)-to-inertial-range correspondence in §04 is standard dimensional analysis; the real inertial range also depends on how isotropic and stationary the flow is.
