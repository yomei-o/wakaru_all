The heart of §01

Almost all of them are integers or half-integers. Only three are not ── \(-5/3\), \(-11/5\) and \(-7/3\).
★ Denominators of 3 and 5. Where do they come from?

02\(-5/3\) is the solution of two linear equations

Kolmogorov's assumption is a single sentence ── "in the inertial range the spectrum is determined by the energy dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) and the wavenumber \(k\) alone". The rest is counting dimensions.

Matching units, and nothing else $$[\varepsilon]=L^2T^{-3},\qquad [k]=L^{-1},\qquad [E(k)]=L^3T^{-2}$$ $$E=\varepsilon^a k^b\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{cases} L:\ 2a-b=3\\ T:\ -3a=-2\end{cases}\ \Longrightarrow\ a=\frac23,\ b=-\frac53$$

We applied the same procedure to other cascades. The simultaneous equations are solved exactly in rational arithmetic (in floating point 2/3 becomes 0.6666… and the pattern disappears).

03The denominators 3 and 5 were a determinant

Every computation above amounts to multiplying by the inverse of a dimensional matrix \(M\). By Cramer's rule the exponents must have the form \((\text{integer})/\det M\).

Kolmogorov's dimensional matrix $$M=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1\\ -3 & 0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det M=2\cdot0-(-1)(-3)=-3$$

The heart of §03 ── the main finding of this episode

The denominator of a spectral exponent is the determinant of the dimensional matrix.

The 3 in \(-5/3\), the 5 in \(-11/5\), the 3 in \(-7/3\) ── all divisors of \(|\det M|\).
And Phillips has \(\det M=1\) ── which is why ocean waves get the integer \(-5\).

Looking inside ──
・Kolmogorov: \(\varepsilon\)'s time dimension \(-3\) becomes \(\det=-3\) directly
・Bolgiano: the temperature constraints (\(\Theta^2\) and \(\Theta^{-1}\)) feed into the time equation to give \(\det=-5\)
・Phillips: with only \(g\) and \(\omega\), \(\det=-1\)

★ This is the claim of this episode

"Spectral slopes leave the integers because the determinant of the dimensional matrix is not 1 ── not because a fractional derivative is hiding in the laws of physics."

Episode 2 said physics is nearly integer-order. Turbulence looks like a counterexample. But ──
★ the governing equations (Navier–Stokes) are still integer-order. The fractions appear after taking statistics ── not in the order, but in the division of dimensions.
Verdict: this is our own framing, not a new claim. But it does confirm that Episode 2's hypothesis is not broken by turbulence.

05The main point ── structure functions give 2/3

What is actually measured in turbulence is not the spectrum but the structure function.

Spectral exponent and structure-function exponent $$S_2(\tau)=\bigl\langle |x(t+\tau)-x(t)|^2\bigr\rangle\propto\tau^{\zeta_2},\qquad \zeta_2=-\beta-1\quad(-3<\beta<-1)$$ $$\beta=-\frac53\ \Longrightarrow\ \zeta_2=\frac23\qquad\textbf{★ Kolmogorov's two-thirds law}$$

Conclusion of §05

Three-digit agreement in the middle of the band (\(\beta=-2\sim-2.2\)). The 0.11 discrepancy at the edge (\(\beta=-1.2\)) is not an error but the slow convergence near the ends of the range \(-3<\beta<-1\).

★ Kolmogorov's two-thirds law is a restatement of the \(-5/3\) law.
Both follow from the single sentence "determined by the energy flux \(\varepsilon\) and a length" ── they are not separate laws.

§06 ── far too many "about \(-1\)"s

\(1/f\) is the commonest exponent in nature. And yet ── ★ it has more than one origin.

The leading account is a log-uniform distribution of relaxation times, but "why log-uniform?" differs case by case (surface states in semiconductors, inflation for the CMB).
It went into Episode 2's list of open doors and is still open.

★ Borrowing Episode 13's language, though, \(1/f\) is the direct consequence of "log-uniform ＝ scale-invariant".
\(\beta=-1\) means \(\alpha=1/2\) and flatness in \(\ln\omega\) ── the exponent that lives closest to the imaginary-order world.

§07 ── it sorts into three

Within this series' scope, there turn out to be only three reasons an exponent leaves the integers:

1. The order itself is fractional (diffusion, Warburg) ── Episodes 2, 11
2. Matching dimensions produces a fraction (turbulence) ── this episode
3. The order goes complex (critical \(1/r^2\)) ── Episode 13

And 1 and 3 are about the form of the law; 2 is about how statistics are taken.
★ Turbulence's \(-5/3\) is the second, not the first ── which is what this episode most wanted to say.

Exercises

  1. In this episode's language, why is the Phillips law for ocean waves the integer \(-5\)?
    Show answer
    Because only two quantities enter, \(g\) (\(LT^{-2}\)) and \(\omega\) (\(T^{-1}\)), giving the dimensional matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}\) with \(\det M=-1\).
    Cramer's denominator is 1, so the exponents must be integers ── whether you get an integer was settled by the determinant from the start.
  2. Rebut the claim "turbulence is evidence that physical law is fractional-order".
    Show answer
    The Navier–Stokes equations are integer-order (first in time, second in space). The fractions appear in the statistically averaged spectrum of their solutions.
    So \(-5/3\) is a property of dimensional division, not of the equation.
    ★ For contrast, the Warburg impedance's \(\alpha=-1/2\) is an equation that really is half-order (the diffusion equation solved at a boundary) ── a genuine fractional order. Same "fraction", different provenance.
  3. How does \(1/f\) noise relate to Episode 13's imaginary-order differentiation?
    Show answer
    Both have the property of being flat or periodic in \(\ln\omega\).
    \(1/f\): constant power per unit interval of \(\ln\omega\) (equal energy per octave).
    Imaginary order: phase proportional to \(\ln\omega\) (periodic in \(\ln\omega\)).
    ★ The former is scale invariance of the amplitude; the latter of the phase ── two sides of the same symmetry.
    This is a correspondence, not an explanation of the origin of \(1/f\).

Summary　Sorting slopes by their provenance

Episode 2's dictionary (\(\beta=-2\alpha\)) puts every slope in this series on one page. Almost all are integers or half-integers; the exceptions are the turbulent \(-5/3\), \(-11/5\) and \(-7/3\).

Tracking down their denominators gives ── ★ the determinant of the dimensional matrix. \(|\det M|=3\) for Kolmogorov, 5 for Bolgiano–Obukhov, and 1 for Phillips, hence the integer \(-5\). Whether you get an integer was decided by a determinant.

We checked numerically too ── generating noise of exponent \(\beta\) and measuring it back (agreement within scatter), and the structure-function exponent \(\zeta_2=-\beta-1\) (three digits in the middle of the band). Kolmogorov's two-thirds law is a restatement of the \(-5/3\) law.

★ There are only three ways for an exponent to leave the integers ──
a fractional order, a division of dimensions, or a complex order.

And turbulence is the second. Navier–Stokes stays integer-order and the fractions appear only after taking statistics.
── Episode 2's "physics is nearly integer-order" survives turbulence intact.
