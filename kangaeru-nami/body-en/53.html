Why the Measurable Range
Is Always Logarithmic Logarithms appeared in ten places across this series — decades, e-folds, \(q^*\), information, proper distance.
★ They share one root — \(f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)\). Its only continuous solution is \(c\ln x\) (confirmed numerically).
★★ And the companion fact — "range" goes as \(\log N\), "precision" as \(N^{-1/2}\). A millionfold more data buys 100× precision but only 3× range.

Exercises

  1. §04 says the only continuous solution is \(c\ln x\). What happens without continuity?
    Show answer
    Infinitely many pathological solutions appear. Setting \(g(u)=f(e^u)\) turns \(f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)\) into Cauchy's additive equation \(g(u+v)=g(u)+g(v)\).
    ★ Impose continuity (or measurability, or local boundedness — any one suffices) and the solution is \(g(u)=cu\), i.e. \(f=c\ln x\).
    ★★ Drop it, and using a basis of \(\mathbb{R}\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\) one constructs everywhere-discontinuous solutions (the axiom of choice is needed). Their graphs are dense in the plane.
    ★ In physics continuity is taken for granted, so effectively "only the logarithm" — but when writing "of course," one should write what was assumed.
  2. How does §07's "range \(\log N\), precision \(N^{-1/2}\)" translate into experiment design?
    Show answer
    First, distinguish "not enough range" from "not enough precision."
    ★ If precision is short, more measuring time helps as \(N^{-1/2}\) — 4× the time for 2× better. Effort is repaid.
    ★★ If range is short, more time helps only as \(\log N\) — 1000× the time for 1.3×. Nearly wasted.
    ★★★ Then you change not the apparatus but the subject — Episode 51's "choose a flow with a wide inertial range," where 6 decades reaches 0.059 dB/oct. As experiment design, that decision comes first.
  3. §02 says "the invariant measure is \(dx/x\), hence the logarithm." Is that an explanation?
    Show answer
    Only half of one. Granted the premise "we want quantities invariant under scale transformations," it does explain why a logarithm appears.
    ★ But it does not explain why physics is scale-invariant in the first place — this article only worked backwards from results.
    ★★ The same shape as Episode 48's conclusion — we learned that the axis works where scale invariance holds, but not where the scale invariance comes from.
    ★ At a critical point there is an explanation (the correlation length diverges and the characteristic length disappears). But "why are so many phenomena near a critical point?" is another question again.

Summary — two faces of the same wall

Logarithms appeared in ten places across the series — Episode 5's ratio to the age of the universe, Episode 21's information, Episode 22's wavelet plane, Episode 39's proper distance, Episode 45's Rindler time, Episodes 46–47's e-folds, and Episodes 51–52's decades and \(q^*\).

★ The only measure invariant under multiplication is \(dx/x\) (confirmed numerically: still \(\ln3\) after a hundredfold stretch). With \(dx/x\) invariant, the natural coordinate is \(\ln x\).
★ And with constant relative resolution, the count of distinguishable steps is proportional to the decades (constant Q, Episode 22) — so "how many decades" is "how many things could be told apart."

★★★ The core — one root: \(f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)\).
Its only continuous solution is \(c\ln x\) (\(p/q\to\ln3/\ln2\) to \(10^{-8}\)).
The moment you decide to turn multiplication into addition, the logarithm is forced.

★ Episode 28 sorted "one source of exponentials is addition becoming multiplication." This is that read backwards. Information (Episode 21) has the same form — side by side, states multiply while information adds.

★ A by-product — for Episode 52's \(q^*\), the textbook criterion (\(f(\alpha)\ge0\)) allows \(q\approx25.9\) while the measurement gives 4: more than six times too optimistic. "One on average" is not "one every time."

★★ And the second core —
"range" goes as \(\log N\), "precision" as \(N^{-1/2}\).
A millionfold more data buys 100× precision but only 3× range.

★ "Measure finer" rewards effort; "measure wider" barely does — that is what Episodes 51 and 52 ran into. ★ Honestly: why physics is scale-invariant is not explained here; we only worked backwards from results.

That the Haar measure of the scaling group is \(dx/x\), that the continuous solutions of Cauchy's equation \(f(xy)=f(x)+f(y)\) are exactly \(c\ln x\), that Shannon information is logarithmic because it must be additive, and the \(f(\alpha)\ge0\) sampling criterion for multifractals are all well-known results and none are discoveries of this article — what this article did was line up the ten logarithms of this series, show they reduce to the same functional equation, and put numbers on the contrast "range is \(\log N\), precision is \(N^{-1/2}\)." §04's "only \(c\ln x\)" assumes continuity (or measurability, or local boundedness); without it, everywhere-discontinuous solutions exist (Exercise 1). The comparison in §06 depends on Episode 52's measurement conditions (\(|\Delta\tau|\le0.1\), median of five seeds); a looser criterion extends \(q^*\). The \(N^{-1/2}\) of §07 assumes independent estimates; correlations slow the improvement.
