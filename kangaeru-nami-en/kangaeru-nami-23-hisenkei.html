The point of §04 — this is decisive

Same frequency, same input, two different answers. Which one you get depends on how you arrived — hysteresis.
Between \(\omega=1.24\) and \(1.42\) the response is two-valued; sweeping up it drops at \(1.44\), sweeping down it jumps at \(1.22\).

★ A transfer function \(H(\omega)\) is a function: put in a frequency, get a response.
With two answers, it cannot be a function at all.

§05 — the best find of this episode

What breaks is only Part I's "filter" picture.
All three constraints of Part IV survive.

The reason is clear — "filter" is a word from linearity, whereas

★ causality, bandwidth and the arrow of time come not from linearity
but from time running in one direction only.

★ And as Episode 21 showed, that one-directionality is the imaginary axis of the complex frequency plane.
Constraints that came from the imaginary axis survive nonlinearity.

§06 — living in a linear world changes how you swim

A sperm cell's world is \(Re\sim10^{-4}\) — completely linear. It cannot coast on inertia; stop the flagellum and it stops instantly.

★ There is a deeper consequence — in a linear world, a time-reversible motion cannot propel you (the scallop theorem).
Opening and closing like a scallop just returns you to where you started. So microorganisms use motions that do not retrace under time reversal, such as a rotating helical flagellum.

★ Episode 10's "the arrow of time lives in the parity of the order" was deciding the shape of living things.

Exercises

  1. Explain the difference between radio "intermodulation" and audio "harmonic distortion" using §02's table.
    Show answer
    Both come from the same squared term, but they land in different places.
    Harmonics (2000, 2200 Hz) are integer multiples of the input — octave-related, so musically tolerable.
    Intermodulation (100, 2100 Hz) is the sum and difference — frequencies unrelated to the input, so they sound dissonant.
    ★ In radio this is how a strong station leaks into a weak one, and §02's 100 Hz difference tone is exactly that.
  2. Why does causality survive nonlinearity?
    Show answer
    Because causality is a condition on time ordering — output does not precede input — and has nothing to do with linearity.
    That said, Episode 8's Kramers–Kronig relations are a theorem about linear response functions and do not carry over unchanged.
    ★ There are nonlinear extensions (dispersion relations for the nonlinear susceptibilities \(\chi^{(2)}, \chi^{(3)}\)) — the structure "causality = analyticity" is preserved order by order.
  3. How does a sperm cell's inability to "swim like a scallop" relate to Episode 10?
    Show answer
    Both are about what happens under time reversal.
    Episode 10: even-order terms are invariant under \(t\to-t\), hence reversible.
    Here: at \(Re\ll1\) the inertial (nonlinear) term vanishes and only the viscous, linear equation remains. That equation is first order in time with time-independent coefficients, so running "open and close" backwards returns you exactly to the start.
    ★ To move forward, the shape change itself must fail to retrace under time reversal — turning a helix, sending a travelling wave.
    ★ The Reynolds number of the fluid was deciding the propulsion mechanism of living things.

Summary — what broke was only the vocabulary of linearity

"Filter" and "transfer function," used since Episode 1, all presupposed superposition. Add nothing but a squared term and — two go in, seven come out (DC, difference, sum, two harmonics). Something a linear filter can never do.

By distortion figures, people notice from about 1 %. The everyday "linear world" was an approximation ignoring nonlinearity at the 0.1 % level — linearity is not a property of nature but a consequence of smallness.

For the Duffing oscillator, the same frequency and the same input give two answers (two-valued from \(\omega=1.24\) to \(1.42\), with a maximum gap of 1.85). With no function to speak of, the word "transfer function" cannot be used.

★ And yet every constraint of Part IV survived —
causality, the bandwidth theorem, the arrow of time, and dimensional analysis.

The reason is clear — "filter" is a word from linearity, while the three constraints come from time running in one direction only. In Episode 21's language: constraints that came from the imaginary axis survive nonlinearity.

Across every field, the strength of nonlinearity is measured by the same dimensionless "nonlinear over linear" (\(Re\), \(n_2I/n_0\), \(GM/rc^2\)). A sperm cell's world is \(Re\sim10^{-4}\), completely linear — so it cannot swim like a scallop and turns a helix instead. And when dispersion and nonlinearity balance, you get a soliton (38 mW for 20 ps).

On the numbers here: the nonlinearity of §02–03 is the simplest possible form, "output is a polynomial in the input" (memoryless nonlinearity); real amplifiers and loudspeakers have nonlinearity with memory (dependent on previous state), so their distortion is more complicated. The THD values are computed from assumed \(a_2, a_3\), not measured from hardware (the "high-end audio" comparisons are order-of-magnitude labels). The Duffing oscillator of §04 is a numerical solution at \(\gamma=0.05,\beta=0.3,F=0.3\), and the jump positions depend strongly on those values. Each sweep point discards 60 periods of transient before measuring the amplitude; a slower sweep moves the jump points slightly. The Reynolds numbers of §06 are estimates from representative values. The scallop theorem (Purcell's theorem) is a rigorous result about low-Reynolds-number flow, but this article only states its content and does not derive it. The soliton condition is the standard fundamental-soliton form of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, using standard optical-fibre values \(\gamma=1.3\times10^{-3}\)/(W·m) and \(\beta_2=-20\) ps²/km. The "survives nonlinearity" verdicts of §05 are this article's organization and are not rigorous proofs item by item — in particular, the nonlinear Kramers–Kronig relations require an order-by-order formulation and are not as simple as the linear case. — To print, use your browser's Print and "Save as PDF."
