01First a check ── does log-uniform really give 1/f?

Superposing first-order (Lorentzian) relaxations $$S(\omega)=\int g(\tau)\,\frac{2\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}\,d\tau,\qquad \text{log-uniform}\ \Leftrightarrow\ g(\tau)=\frac{A}{\tau}$$

In this case the integral can be done by hand

The result $$\int \frac{A}{\tau}\cdot\frac{2\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}\,d\tau=\frac{2A}{\omega}\Big[\arctan\omega\tau\Big]\ \xrightarrow[\ \omega\tau_1\ll1\ll\omega\tau_2\ ]{}\ \frac{\pi A}{\omega}$$

Checked numerically (\(\tau\in[10^{-6},10^{6}]\) s, 12 decades, sliced logarithmically).

The heart of §01

Log-uniform ⇒ 1/f, confirmed (slope exactly −1 inside the band, amplitude matching \(\pi A/\omega\) to 6 digits).
★ And outside the band it flattens ── 1/f always has an upper and a lower corner. It does not continue forever.

02★ The real question ── where does log-uniform come from?

Looked at directly in \(\tau\), \(g(\tau)\propto1/\tau\) seems like a special shape. But ── change one variable and it becomes the most ordinary shape there is.

Thermally activated processes (Arrhenius) $$\tau=\tau_0\,e^{E/kT}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ln\tau=\ln\tau_0+\frac{E}{kT}$$

\(\ln\tau\) is a linear function of the barrier \(E\)

Write the Jacobian $$g(\tau)\,d\tau=D(E)\,dE,\qquad dE=kT\frac{d\tau}{\tau}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\ g(\tau)=\frac{kT\,D(E)}{\tau}\ }$$

If \(D\) is flat, you get \(1/\tau\) directly

The heart of §02

★★ \(E\) uniform ⟺ \(\ln\tau\) uniform ⟺ \(g(\tau)\propto1/\tau\)

No power-law distribution needs arranging. All that is needed is "whatever sits in the exponent is flat" ── an entirely ordinary situation.

How many decades does that buy (\(\tau_0=10^{-13}\) s, room temperature 300 K)? The barrier per decade is \(kT\ln10=0.05953\) eV.

★ Six decades of 1/f cost only 0.36 eV of scatter in the barrier. That is far too cheap.
And with \(E_{\max}=1.0\) eV, \(\tau_{\max}=1.75\) hours ── 1/f should turn over below \(2.5\times10^{-5}\) Hz. If an experiment sees no turnover down to \(10^{-6}\) Hz, the barriers reach beyond 1.3 eV.

03Why "1"? ── the exponent is a ratio of two decade-counts

What if \(D(E)\) is not flat? Take \(D(E)\propto e^{cE}\) and let \(D\) vary by 10× and 1000× across the band.

★ Why the "1" in 1/f does not move $$\beta-1=kT\,\frac{d\ln D}{dE}=\frac{\text{decades over which }D\text{ varies}}{\text{decades over which }\tau\text{ spreads}}$$

The heart of §03

Here \(\tau\) spreads over 11.8 decades, so a 10× variation in \(D\) gives \(\beta-1=1/11.8=0.085\), and even 1000× gives only 0.255.
★★ The denominator is large, so \(\beta\) pins itself near 1.
That measured 1/f exponents scatter over \(\beta=0.8\)–\(1.4\) is exactly this ratio. ── ★ 1/f is not the result of fine-tuning. It is harder to avoid.

★ What vanished in Episode 13 was the impulse response of an imaginary-order filter (crushed by \(e^{-\beta\pi}\)).

Exercises

  1. In some systems the 1/f exponent \(\beta\) approaches 1 as the temperature rises, in others it moves away. How does the formula here read that?
    Show the answer
    Because both sides of \(\beta-1=kT\,d\ln D/dE\) move with temperature. The numerator \(kT\) grows with \(T\), so if \(D(E)\) is sloped, \(\beta\) moves away from 1.
    On the other hand, raising \(T\) shifts the barrier window that matters at the observation frequency, \(E^\ast=kT\ln(1/\omega\tau_0)\), to higher barriers ── and if \(D\) happens to be flat there, \(\beta\) moves toward 1.
    ★ So measuring \(\beta(T)\) amounts to scanning \(D(E)\) ── this is the idea behind the Dutta–Horn analysis. This article only confirmed the shape numerically; it analysed no measured data.
  2. What follows from a report that 1/f continues without a turnover down to \(10^{-6}\) Hz?
    Show the answer
    That the barriers extend beyond 1.3 eV. With \(\tau_{\max}=\tau_0e^{E_{\max}/kT}\), \(\tau_0=10^{-13}\) s at 300 K: \(E=1.0\) eV gives 1.75 hours (\(2.5\times10^{-5}\) Hz), \(E=1.3\) eV gives \(6.9\times10^{8}\) s.
    ★ And "1/f continuing forever" does not exist ── the total power \(\int(1/f)df\) diverges logarithmically, so there must be a corner somewhere. Not seeing it just means the measurement was not long enough.
  3. Episode 13 concluded that log-periodicity is hard to see, yet here a 3 dB ripple appears. Is that a contradiction?
    Show the answer
    Not a contradiction ── they measure different things. What was crushed in Episode 13 was the impulse response of an imaginary-order filter, whose amplitude falls as \(e^{-\beta\pi}\) (\(10^{-6}\) at \(\beta=3\)).
    What survives here is the periodic undulation a discrete ladder of time constants carves into the spectrum, of size \(2/\cosh(\pi^2/\ln\lambda)\).
    ★ The first is a log-oscillation made by a continuous imaginary order; the second is one made by discrete real orders ── both live on the \(\ln\omega\) axis, but they come from different places. And the second is much easier to find.

Summary　What nature had ready was not a power law but an exponential

We answered the question Episode 26 left ── "why are log-uniform distributions of time constants so common in nature?"

First, log-uniform ⇒ 1/f confirmed (slope exactly −1 inside the band, amplitude matching \(\pi A/\omega\) to 6 digits). Outside the band it flattens ── 1/f always has an upper and a lower corner.

★ And the answer was the exponential map. With \(\tau=\tau_0e^{E/kT}\), \(\ln\tau\) is linear in \(E\), so \(g(\tau)=kT\,D(E)/\tau\) ── a flat \(D\) alone gives \(1/\tau\). No power-law distribution needs arranging. The barrier per decade is 0.0595 eV at room temperature, so 0.36 eV of scatter buys 6 decades of 1/f.

★★ Why the exponent pins near 1 came out too ── \(\beta-1\) = (decades over which \(D\) varies) ÷ (decades over which \(\tau\) spreads). Even a 1000× variation in \(D\) gives \(\beta-1=0.25\). The 0.8–1.4 scatter of measured exponents is exactly this ratio.

\(\alpha\) decay is this map at its most extreme: \(Q\) varies by 2.19× while the half-life varies by 24.2 decades (Geiger–Nuttall, \(R^2=0.9955\)) ── which is why Episode 19's decay constants fill in continuously.

★ And one prediction failed. Discrete scale invariance (\(\tau_n=\tau_0\lambda^n\)) does make 1/f, but at the Efimov ratio 22.69 it leaves a 2.95 dB log-periodic ripple (matching \(2/\cosh(\pi^2/\ln\lambda)\) to 3 digits). What was crushed in Episode 13 was the imaginary-order impulse response ── a different thing. Turned around, this is a testable prediction ── 1/f flat to 0.1 dB means a ladder ratio of at most 4.5, i.e. a continuous distribution behind it.

★ 1/f is not the result of fine-tuning but a phenomenon that is hard to avoid.
Any system whose rates are exponentials of ordinary quantities becomes log-uniform on its own.

★★ And Episode 26's door closes ── Episode 26: "fractional order ⇐ scale-invariant degrees of freedom"; Episode 27: "scale-invariant degrees of freedom ⇐ the exponential map". The origin of fractional order was the exponential function.
