How much of Episode 42's \(f(\alpha)\) curve can actually be measured?

Extracting the Order's Distribution from Finite Data —
Only the tails are out of reach Episode 42 said "the order is not one number but a distribution." ★ But can the shape of that distribution actually be measured?
★ Multiply the number of boxes by a thousand (1,024 → 1,048,576) and the reachable \(q\) grows only from 2 to 4.
★★ The peak of \(f(\alpha)\) is always measurable; only the two tails are out of reach in principle — and the tails are the interesting part.

The point of §04

★ The peak of \(f(\alpha)\) (\(q=0\), \(f=1\)) is always measurable — it is the dimension of the support.
★★ What is not measurable is both tails — the strongest and the weakest regions.
★★★ And those are the interesting part. Intermittency is about rare, violent events — and being rare is exactly why they are not in your sample.

The point of §05 — ★ where our prediction failed

★ \(q=0\) is completely unaffected by noise (it only counts boxes). For small noise (\(s\le0.3\)) nothing moves much.
★★★ We initially predicted "negative \(q\) will be the most fragile" — and were wrong. At \(s=1.0\) the shifts are 0.24 (6 %) at \(q=-2\), 0.37 (18 %) at \(q=4\), 0.62 (19 %) at \(q=6\) — the positive side moves more.
★ Why: with log-normal noise the large values scatter more in absolute terms, so positive \(q\) breaks first.
★★ Either way it is large \(|q|\) that suffers — the same direction as §03's conclusion.

Exercises

  1. §02 notes that the cascade was changed to a non-conservative one. Why?
    Show answer
    Because in the conservative version the sampling fluctuation vanishes entirely. If each level always hands out \(p\) and \(1-p\) as a pair, the final multiset of box values is identical every time (\(C(n,k)\) boxes at \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\)).
    ★ Then \(Z(q)=(p^q+(1-p)^q)^n\) holds exactly and \(\tau_{\rm emp}\) always equals the exact answer — indeed the first version did exactly that and showed zero departure.
    ★★ The departure exists only when the multiset of values fluctuates, not merely their arrangement. Hence the switch to drawing each child independently.
    ★ A practical lesson about choosing models — check that the effect you are studying has not been erased by how you built the model.
  2. §03 found \(q^*\) growing only logarithmically. What is the physical reason?
    Show answer
    Because the probability of the box that dominates the \(q\)-th moment falls exponentially. The larger \(q\), the more extreme the dominating box, and its chance of appearing rises in proportion to the number of boxes.
    ★ So reaching "one level deeper into the extremes" requires a fixed multiple more boxes — the logarithm of the box count sets \(q^*\).
    ★★ Indeed \(n\) (\(=\log_2\) of the count) going 10 → 20 takes \(q^*\) from 2 to 4, roughly proportional to \(n\).
    ★ This is exactly Episode 51's "decades" again — both have the logarithm of the available data as their ceiling.
  3. §04 says the interesting ends are unreachable. What does one do in practice?
    Show answer
    Three things.
    ★ ① Fit a model and extrapolate: fit \(\tau(q)\) over the reachable range and get the ends from the model. But that is "assumed," not "measured" — per Episode 43, say which.
    ★ ② Measure something else that is sensitive to the tails: extreme-value statistics (the distribution of maxima), for instance, reaches the tail with fewer samples than moments do.
    ★ ③ Do not claim the ends: stop at "\(\tau(q)\) bends" — as §06 says, that suffices for Episode 42's claim.
    ★★ This series' default is ③, and if ① is used it must be labelled "a model assumption."

Summary — "it is a distribution" is sayable; "which distribution" is not

We asked whether Episode 42's \(f(\alpha)\) curve can actually be extracted from finite data.

Measuring \(\tau(q)\) from a single realization, small \(|q|\) agrees and large \(q\) departs — with an inconsistent sign, because large \(q\) is decided by a single strongest box. Only \(q=0\) is exactly \(-1\) (it just counts boxes).

★★★ The core —
a thousandfold increase in boxes (1,024 → 1M) moves \(q^*\) only from 2 to 4.
The \(\alpha\) width goes 54.5 % → 89.4 % and stops.

★ \(q^*\) grows logarithmically; reaching the tails needs exponentially more data.
★★ The peak of \(f(\alpha)\) (the support dimension) is always measurable; only the tails are not — intermittency is about rare, violent events, and being rare is exactly why they are missing from the sample.

★ One prediction failed: we expected negative \(q\) to be the most noise-fragile, but the positive side moved more (at \(s=1.0\): 6 % at \(q=-2\), 19 % at \(q=6\)) — with log-normal noise the large values scatter more.

★ Conclusion — Episode 42's "the order was a distribution" survives finite data.
But only as far as "it is a distribution."
Which distribution is decided by the size of your sample.

On the numbers here: the cascade generation, the estimation of \(\tau(q)\) from a single realization, the measurement of \(q^*\) (median over five seeds), the reachable portion of \(f(\alpha)\), and the effect of added noise were all computed here. The multifractal formalism (\(\tau(q)\), the Legendre transform, \(f(\alpha)\)), multiplicative cascades, and the fact that in a finite sample the high-order moments are dominated by extremes so that \(\tau(q)\) linearizes are all well-known results and none are discoveries of this article — what this article did was connect them to this series' handbook (Episodes 49 and 51) and write "the \(q\) range" as an indicator of the same kind as the decade count. As noted in §02, a conservative cascade (handing out \(p\) and \(1-p\) in pairs) produces an identical multiset of box values every time, erasing the sampling fluctuation; this article therefore draws each child's multiplier independently and normalizes. That choice affects the results. The criterion \(|\Delta\tau|\le0.1\) defining \(q^*\) is a convention adopted here, and a different criterion gives different values. The \(q^*\) figures in §03 are medians over five seeds; the scatter from one realization to the next is larger. This article too used only synthetic data and was not applied to any real measurement. — To print, use your browser's Print and "Save as PDF."
