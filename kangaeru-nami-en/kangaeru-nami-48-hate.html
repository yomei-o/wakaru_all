Thinking in Waves Episode 48 / Part XVII — Looking for the Edge of the Axis

Where your own tool stops working

The Edge of the Order Axis —
There is exactly one condition for using it Episode 43's exercises said a third test would come from somewhere with no power law at all. ★ Here we go looking for it deliberately.
★ Measuring the local order \(\alpha(\omega)=d\ln|H|/d\ln\omega\) — an exponential gives \(\alpha=-\omega\), with no upper bound (it runs off the scale).
★★ And a marginal coupling approaches 0 only as \(1/\ln\mu\) — still \(-0.0130\) after 32 decades. That is not "a power law of order 0."

The point of §04 — ★★★

"Marginal" does not mean the order is 0.
It means the order approaches 0 logarithmically.

★ After 32 decades it is still \(-0.0130\) — not "a power law of order 0."
★★ \(\alpha\times\ln\mu\) approaching \(-1/b=-1\) is the signature of a \(1/\ln\mu\) form.
★ Everything physics calls a marginal coupling — \(\phi^4\) in four dimensions, QCD's \(\alpha_s\), weak localization in 2D (Episode 37) — lives here. That is why logarithms appear.

05★ Edge 3 — in a finite system, not even an exponent is definable

The point of §07 — ★★★ the conclusion

None of these means the axis is wrong.
They make its condition of use explicit —
that scale invariance holds over some number of decades.

★ And the reason power laws are so common in physics is that this condition is so often met — near a critical point, between the correlation length and the system size, in the inertial range, outside a horizon. All of them regions with no characteristic length.
★★ Turned around — let a single characteristic length in, and the axis is at best a local coordinate.

Exercises

  1. §02 had the exponential's \(\alpha\) diverge. Does that mean an exponential "has no order"?
    Show answer
    Not "none" but "no single value." At each point \(\alpha(\omega)=-\omega\) is perfectly well defined; definability is not the problem.
    ★ The problem is that the range over which it stays the same is nearly zero (0.087 decades in §03) — the summary called "order" has stopped being useful.
    ★★ Episode 1 introduced dB/oct precisely because one number could summarize a spectrum. ★ Applying a summary to something unsummarizable only loses information. To summarize an exponential, use its time constant, not an order.
  2. §04 found "marginal" to be \(1/\ln\mu\). How does that differ from Episode 42's "the order becomes a distribution"?
    Show answer
    Episode 42 was "at the same place, different moments give different answers." Different points carry different orders.
    ★ §04 here is "every moment gives the same answer, but that answer drifts slowly with \(\mu\)" — a drift, not a spread.
    ★★ They are distinguishable by measurement — a distribution bends the higher moments (Episode 42); a drift moves every moment alike, as \(\ln\mu\).
    ★ And both can of course happen at once — which this article does not treat.
  3. §07 said power laws appear only where there is no characteristic length. So what have you learned when you see one?
    Show answer
    That no characteristic length or time lies within that range of decades. That is surprisingly strong information.
    ★ If \(1/f\) holds for six decades (Episode 2), then no special time constant lives anywhere in those six decades — a single relaxation process cannot explain it, so a superposition of many is required.
    ★★ Episode 27's ratio that sets \(\beta\) is the same reasoning — how many decades it holds is what narrows down the mechanism.
    ★ Conversely, calling something a power law when it holds for one decade says almost nothing — recall that the exponential in §03 managed 0.087.

Summary — the axis is not wrong; its conditions are now explicit

We went looking for the third test promised in Episode 43's exercises. Measuring the local order \(\alpha(\omega)=d\ln|H|/d\ln\omega\) —

★ The Lorentzian moves continuously from 0 to \(-2\), returning to a power law only at its ends (5.36 decades each).
★★ The exponential gives \(\alpha=-\omega\) with no upper bound — it does not fail to sit on the axis; it runs off the scale. At any target order it earns only 0.087 decades.

★★★ And what "marginal" really is —
not an order of 0, but an order approaching 0 logarithmically.
Still \(-0.0130\) after 32 decades.

In a finite system not even an exponent survives — in the 1D Ising chain at \(L=16\), the decay rate hits 0 at \(r=8\) and goes negative beyond. The spatial version of Episode 5.

Critical exponents too: at \(\epsilon=1\) (three dimensions) the first-order ε-expansion is off by 7.4 % in \(\nu\) and 49.0 % in \(\eta\) — Episode 30's \(\alpha=(d-3)/2\) was a free-field statement, and interactions make the scale non-linear.

★★★ The conclusion — none of these three endings means the axis is wrong.
They make its condition of use explicit:
scale invariance holding over some number of decades.

★ And power laws are common in physics because that condition is so often met — near critical points, in the inertial range, outside horizons: regions with no characteristic length.
★ Honestly: a replacement for the axis — one coordinate that works more widely — was not found here.
