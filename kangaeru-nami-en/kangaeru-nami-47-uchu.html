01★★ The Mukhanov–Sasaki equation is Episode 13's equation

The equation for primordial fluctuations $$v''+\left(k^2-\frac{z''}{z}\right)v=0,\qquad \frac{z''}{z}=\frac{\nu^2-\frac14}{\tau^2}\ \ (\nu=\tfrac32\ \text{is exact de Sitter})$$
★ Put \(x=-k\tau\) and \(k\) disappears $$\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}+\left[1-\frac{\nu^2-\frac14}{x^2}\right]v=0$$

The point of §01

★ \(k\) dropping out is what scale invariance really is. Every wavelength obeys the same equation, so every wavelength comes out with the same amplitude.
★★ And for \(x\ll1\) (outside the horizon) the 1 stops mattering — \(v_{xx}=\dfrac{\nu^2-\frac14}{x^2}v\), exactly Episode 13's \(1/r^2\) potential. \(v\sim x^s\) with \(s=\frac12\pm\nu\).

★★ Once \(x<1\), \(|\zeta|\) becomes constant (1.41 → 1.005 → 1.00005) — that is "freezing."

★★ In wave language, a wave that can no longer travel — a cousin of Episode 28's evanescent wave. There it could not travel in space and decayed exponentially; here it cannot keep up in time and simply stops.

The two most precisely measured "tilts away from ideal" in physics
are both of order 0.1 dB/oct.

★ It is a coincidence — the two have no common origin. This article only says that put on the same scale, they land near each other.
★★ What they do share is that the departure itself carries the physics — for the universe, that inflation ended in finite time; for turbulence, that the cascade multiplies.

Exercises

  1. §05 placed two "0.1 dB/oct" side by side. Is that a meaningful coincidence?
    Show answer
    No — it is a coincidence, as the article says. The universe's \(n_s\) comes from inflation ending in finite time; turbulence's bent \(\zeta_p\) comes from the cascade being multiplicative. There is no shared origin.
    ★ What can be said is that they can be put on the same scale. Episode 43's table gains another row.
    ★★ That "almost zero but not zero" appeared twice is suggestive — both mean an ideal symmetry is slightly broken. ★ But going further requires a mechanism linking them, and this article has none.
  2. §03 froze \(\zeta\). Why does freezing preserve information?
    Show answer
    Because what does not change is a record. Outside the horizon nothing can reach it causally, so there is nothing left to change \(\zeta\).
    ★ In wave language it is "a mode with \(\omega\to0\)" — the same place Episode 4 put the constant of integration. \(\omega=0\) means time-independent, i.e. a constant.
    ★★ Episode 46 had the same \(\omega\to0\) mean "unmeasurable." Here it means "indelible." ★ The difference is whether the horizon later expands to bring it back into view — in inflation it does; for a black hole it does not.
  3. §01 said \(k\) dropping out is what scale invariance is. Then where does \(n_s\ne1\) come from?
    Show answer
    From \(z''/z\) not being exactly \(2/\tau^2\). In exact de Sitter (\(H\) perfectly constant) \(\nu=3/2\), \(k\) vanishes completely and \(n_s=1\).
    ★ But if \(H\) decreases slowly, \(\nu\) drifts from \(3/2\) and some \(k\)-dependence returns — \(n_s-1=3-2\nu\).
    ★★ So \(n_s\ne1\) is a direct measurement of "inflation was not exact de Sitter." Exact de Sitter would go on forever, so to end at all it must have been off. ★ If \(n_s=1\) held precisely, that would be the awkward result — a worked example of Episode 15's "a miss carries more information."

Summary — the universe's tilt is \(-0.105\) dB/oct

The Mukhanov–Sasaki equation is Episode 13's \(1/\tau^2\) potential itself — put \(x=-k\tau\) and it becomes \(v_{xx}+[1-(\nu^2-\frac14)/x^2]v=0\), ★ with \(k\) gone. That is what scale invariance is. Outside the horizon, \(v\sim x^{1/2\pm\nu}\).

★★★ Episode 13 (the critical potential), Episode 39 (AdS) and this episode (inflation)
are three faces of the same quadratic.

Solving it confirmed \(P(k)\propto k^{3-2\nu}\) to \(10^{-6}\) — \(n_s=1\) at \(\nu=3/2\). And once \(x<1\), \(|\zeta|\) goes constant and freezes (1.41 → 1.005 → 1.00005) — the same place Episode 4 put the constant of integration (\(\omega=0\)), which is why it survives to 380,000 years.

Against observation, \(V\propto\phi^2\) passes on \(n_s\) but is excluded by \(r=0.133\), while Starobinsky (\(n_s=0.9667\), \(r=0.0033\)) passes both. ★ The lesson of Episode 42 again — one matching exponent is not enough.

★★★ And the core — put \(n_s=0.9649\) on Episode 1's scale and it reads
\(3(n_s-1)=-0.1053\pm0.0126\) dB/oct,
the same order as Episode 42's \(+0.0812\) for turbulence.

★ A coincidence with no shared origin — the article says so. ★ What they share is that the departure itself carries the physics — for the universe, that inflation ended in finite time; for turbulence, that the cascade multiplies. An \(n_s\) of exactly 1 would mean inflation never ended.

★ And inflation is Episodes 44 and 45's parametric drive — \(v''+\omega^2(t)v=0\), \(|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=1\). The CMB fluctuations are the dynamical-Casimir photons of an expanding universe. Only one thing differs: nobody is doing the shaking — the expansion does it, funded by the inflaton's potential energy.

The e-fold estimate in §06 assumes one reheating temperature and is very rough; the actual \(N\) depends on the details of reheating. The pass/fail marks on \(n_s\) in §04 use a \(3\sigma\) criterion chosen here for convenience; real model selection is done by joint fits.
