Six names, one identity

What that formula is called ──
the action of a classical path in imaginary time Does Episode 62’s \(G=\frac{2}{\hbar}\int\sqrt{2m(V-E)}\,dr\) have a name? ── At least six, spanning 140 years.
★★★ And the deepest name gives away what it is: that integral is the action of a classical path in imaginary time, \(S_E\), with \(G=2S_E/\hbar\). Rotate time by 90° and the barrier becomes a valley the particle simply rolls through. Here we check that numerically (ratio 1.000000).
★★ Further ── Arrhenius and Gamow are two limits of one formula, meeting at \(T_c=\hbar\omega_b/2\pi k_B\). And that \(2\pi\) is the same \(2\pi\) as in the Unruh temperature.

The core of §01

★ One integral collected six names over 140 years. Liouville and Green wrote it as “the asymptotics of a second-order differential equation” 89 years before quantum mechanics.
★★★ The deepest name is the last ── the integral itself is the action of a classical path in imaginary time, \(S_E\), and the tunnelling amplitude is \(e^{-S_E/\hbar}\). That is an instanton.
★★ So tunnelling is classical motion in imaginary time.

02Why \(2/\hbar\)?

where the 2 comes from $$\psi\sim e^{-S_E/\hbar},\qquad P=|\psi|^2=e^{-2S_E/\hbar}=e^{-G}\ \Longrightarrow\ G=\frac{2S_E}{\hbar}$$

★ The 2 in Episode 62’s \(2/\hbar\) is there because the amplitude is squared to get a probability. In decades: the amplitude is suppressed by \(G/(2\ln10)\), the probability by \(G/\ln10\).
★★ Episode 58’s Bogoliubov coefficient \(|\beta|\) is also an amplitude ── which is why its exponent was half. The factor-of-two offset is only about whether you are looking at an amplitude or a probability.

03★★★ In imaginary time, the barrier becomes a valley

rotate time by 90° $$m\ddot x=-V'(x)\ \xrightarrow{\ t=-i\tau\ }\ m\frac{d^2x}{d\tau^2}=+V'(x)$$

The sign flips. In imaginary time the particle moves in \(-V\), the upside-down potential ── where the barrier is a valley and the forbidden region can be crossed classically.

Using energy conservation \(\frac{m}{2}(dx/d\tau)^2=V-E\), that path’s Euclidean action is

and this is our integral $$S_E=\int\left[\frac{m}{2}\left(\frac{dx}{d\tau}\right)^2+V-E\right]d\tau=\int 2(V-E)\,d\tau=\int\sqrt{2m(V-E)}\,dx$$

★ Let us check that by actually solving the inverted-potential equation of motion (\(V=V_0\,\mathrm{sech}^2(x/a)\), \(\hbar=m=1\), \(V_0=1\), \(a=3\)).

The core of §03 ── the summit of this episode

★★★ Agreement to six decimal places. This is not algebra rewritten: it is the result of running the particle through the inverted potential with RK4 and accumulating the action.
★★ “Classically forbidden” was a statement about real time. Rotate time by 90° and the barrier is a valley the particle simply rolls through.
★ The path returns to where it started, which is why it is called a bounce. ★★★ Looking for “the moment of crossing” in tunnelling finds nothing because it is not an event in real time.

The core of §05 ── what to fix in Episode 62’s calculator

★★★ At \(E=V_0\), \(e^{-G}=1\) (everything passes) is plainly wrong. Exact is 0.546; Kemble gives 0.500.
★★ Above the barrier \(e^{-G}\) reaches 784 ── a probability greater than one.

★★★ Why it breaks at the top: the two turning points merge. Episode 57’s “edge” ── we have left the range where the expansion applies. There we counted decades lost as \((1/n)\log(1/\varepsilon)\); here, near the edge, the approximation itself collapses.

06★★★ Arrhenius and Gamow are two limits of one formula

just add them up $$\Gamma(T)\propto\int dE\;\underbrace{e^{-E/k_BT}}_{\text{thermal}}\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+e^{G(E)}}}_{\text{tunnelling}},\qquad G=\frac{2\pi(V_0-E)}{\hbar\omega_b}$$

The exponent is \(-E/k_BT+2\pi E/(\hbar\omega_b)+\text{const}\). The boundary is where the coefficient of \(E\) changes sign:

crossover temperature $$\frac{1}{k_BT_c}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar\omega_b}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;T_c=\frac{\hbar\omega_b}{2\pi k_B}\;}$$

What an experiment measures is the apparent activation energy \(E_{\text{eff}}=-d(\ln\Gamma)/d(1/T)\).

The core of §06 ── the experimental signature of tunnelling

★★★ At low temperature \(E_{\text{eff}}\) is only 3 % of the true barrier. Lowering the temperature stops slowing the rate ── that is the sign that tunnelling has taken over.
★★ The Arrhenius plot (\(\ln\Gamma\) versus \(1/T\)) flattens at low temperature. When the apparent activation energy comes out below the true barrier, the difference is the tunnelling.
★ At high temperature \(E_{\text{eff}}\to V_0+k_BT\) ── slightly above the true barrier, by the \(k_BT\) of the prefactor (1.323 against a predicted 1.375 at \(T=5T_c\)).
★★★ Episode 56 discussed activation as \(\tau=\tau_0e^{E/kT}\) ── that \(E\) was an apparent activation energy. Sweep the temperature, find the knee, and you have \(T_c\) and hence \(\omega_b\).

07That \(2\pi\) is the Unruh \(2\pi\)

★ At temperature \(T\), imaginary time \(\tau\) is a circle of circumference \(\hbar/k_BT\). That is the correspondence between temperature and imaginary time — the same framework as Episode 45’s Unruh effect.

★★ A bounce near a parabolic top has period \(2\pi/\omega_b\). It fits on that circle only if \(\hbar/k_BT\ge2\pi/\omega_b\), i.e. \(T\le T_c\).

08★★★ Perturbation theory cannot see \(e^{-S/\hbar}\) ── but its divergence knows it

The core of §08 ── the deepest point here

★ The series does not converge. \(a_n\sim3^n\,\Gamma(n+\tfrac12)\): factorial divergence.
★★ So perturbation theory can never produce \(e^{-1/(3g)}\) ── its Taylor expansion vanishes at every order. A function that is zero to all orders. ★ That is what “non-perturbative” means.
★★★ And yet the divergence rate \(3^n\) has a “3” that is exactly \(1/S\) for the bounce action \(S=1/(3g)\).
★★★ “Perturbation theory cannot see tunnelling — but the way it fails knows about it.” The quantitative form of Dyson’s argument (1952), computed by Bender–Wu (1969).
★★ This is the main reason that integral collected six names ── it is very nearly the only tool for counting what lies outside perturbation theory.

Exercises

  1. §03 says tunnelling is classical motion in imaginary time. So how long does the crossing take?
    Show answer
    The question is framed in real time, and as posed it has no answer.
    ★ The “duration” \(\tau\) in imaginary time is computable (this episode’s code produces it), but it is not a number of seconds.
    ★★ Real-time tunnelling time still has several competing definitions and no settled answer (Wigner time, Larmor time, Büttiker–Landauer time, …). “How long was the particle inside the barrier” is hard to translate into an observable in the first place.
    ★★★ In this series’ language: it is the same type of problem as Episode 60’s “trend or signal is decided by the record, not the system”. “Time spent inside the barrier” is undefined until you declare how you measure it.
    ★ All this episode claims is that the actions agree ── it says nothing about time.
  2. §05 introduced the Kemble form. Should Episode 62’s calculator be fixed?
    Show answer
    It depends on the use.
    ★ For Episode 62’s purposes, no. Fusion, cold fusion, \(\alpha\) decay and STM all sat at \(G\gg1\). At \(G=10\) the difference between \(e^{-G}\) and \(1/(1+e^G)\) is below \(10^{-4}\).
    ★★ It matters near the barrier top: in Episode 62’s table the riskiest row is d+t at 20 keV (\(G=7.7\)), where the difference is about 0.05 % ── still fine.
    ★★★ Where it really bites is chemistry ── at room temperature \(G\sim1\) is common, and there \(e^{-G}\) is wrong by more than a factor of two. ★ The handbook entry: use Kemble when \(G<5\). Not a blanket rewrite, but counting where the difference appears first ── Episode 55’s discipline.
  3. §08 says the divergence knows the action. Is that a coincidence?
    Show answer
    Not a coincidence: two views of the same integral.
    ★ Dyson’s argument (1952): for \(g<0\) the system is unstable, so \(E(g)\) cannot be analytic at \(g=0\); the radius of convergence is zero and the series must diverge.
    ★★ The degree of that instability — the decay rate \(\sim e^{-S/g}\) — is what sets the divergence rate. Under Borel transformation, \(e^{-S/g}\) corresponds to a singularity in the complex Borel plane, located at \(S\). The \(n!/S^n\) growth is precisely the distance to that singularity.
    ★★★ So “how the perturbation series breaks” and “how big the non-perturbative effect is” are the same information ── organised into the theory of resurgence.
    ★ In this series’ terms: Episode 57 said “an edge is set by its leading correction term”. Here the edge of a series (\(n\to\infty\)) reports the action of a barrier in real space. The edge of the series and the edge of the potential point at the same number.

Summary — six names, one identity

“Does that formula have a name?” ── At least six, over 140 years. Liouville–Green (1837), Jeffreys (1923), WKB (1926), the Gamow factor (1928), Kemble (1935), instanton / bounce (1977).

★★★ And its identity ──
the action of a classical path in imaginary time, \(S_E\), with \(G=2S_E/\hbar\).

★ Rotating time by 90° inverts the potential and turns the barrier into a valley. Running the particle through that inverted potential with RK4 and accumulating the action reproduces the WKB integral to six decimal places. ★★ “Classically forbidden” was a statement about real time.

★★ Against the exact Eckart solution, WKB’s error lives in the prefactor, not the exponent ── the ratio holds at 1.20 across eight decades of transmission (1.5 % spread), matching the analytic \(\exp(\pi[2a\sqrt{2V_0}-\sqrt{8V_0a^2-1}])=1.2041\). That is why Episode 62 could claim the decades.

★ At the barrier top \(e^{-G}\) breaks (1 at \(E=V_0\), 784 at \(1.5V_0\)). Kemble’s \(1/(1+e^G)\) is uniformly valid and exact for a parabolic barrier. It breaks because the two turning points merge ── Episode 57’s edge.

★★★ Arrhenius and Gamow are two limits of one formula, meeting at \(T_c=\hbar\omega_b/2\pi k_B\). The apparent activation energy runs from \(V_0+k_BT\) at \(T=5T_c\) down to 3 % of the true barrier at \(0.3T_c\). ★★ A flattening Arrhenius plot is the experimental signature of tunnelling.

★★ And that \(2\pi\) is the Unruh \(2\pi\) ── both are the circumference of imaginary time. Episode 45 and this episode are two faces of one geometry. ★ Though only the \(2\pi\)’s origin is shared; the physics differs (the same caution as Episode 63).

★★★ And the deepest point: the series \(E(g)=\sum a_ng^n\) diverges as \(a_n\sim3^n\Gamma(n+\frac12)\) and never converges. Perturbation theory can never produce \(e^{-1/(3g)}\) — it is zero to all orders. Yet the “3” in that divergence is exactly \(1/S\) for the bounce action \(S=1/(3g)\) (ratio \(/(-3)=39.5376\) at \(n=40\) against a predicted 39.5). Perturbation theory cannot see tunnelling; the way it fails knows about it.

Next time §08 ended with “the edge of a series points at the same number as the edge of a potential”.
★ Episode 57 counted how an edge is set by its leading correction; this episode found that a series’ edge knows a non-perturbative effect.
★★ Next we join the two ── where is the best place to truncate an asymptotic series? ★ Optimal truncation, and the error left behind being exactly \(e^{-S/\hbar}\) (superasymptotics). It may be the final form of this series’ question: how many decades can you buy?
