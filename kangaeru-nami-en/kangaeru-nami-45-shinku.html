01Subtracting an infinity — possible because the divergence is a volume term

Between walls a distance \(a\) apart the modes are \(\omega_n=n\pi c/a\), and \(E=\frac\hbar2\sum\omega_n\) diverges. Insert a cutoff \(e^{-\delta n}\) and see what diverges.

The point of §01

\(\sum n e^{-\delta n}=\dfrac1{4\sinh^2(\delta/2)}=\dfrac1{\delta^2}-\dfrac1{12}+O(\delta^2)\).
★★ The divergent term \(1/\delta^2\) is proportional to \(a\) — a volume term, identical with or without the walls. That is why it can be subtracted.
★ What remains is \(-1/12\) — finite, and negative.
★★ We did not "subtract an infinity"; we took the difference between having walls and not having them. That is the correct reading. As in Episode 7's "only dimensionless quantities are physics," only what can be compared carries meaning.

Result for a 1D scalar field $$E(a)=-\frac{\pi\hbar c}{24a},\qquad F=-\frac{dE}{da}=-\frac{\pi\hbar c}{24a^2}\quad(\text{attractive})$$

The point of §08 — ★ the caveat that matters most

§01 said the divergence can be subtracted, but only because we took the difference between having walls and not.
★★ Gravity responds to the absolute value, not a difference — so asking for the vacuum's energy density itself leaves nothing to subtract against.
★ That is the cosmological constant problem, and these tools cannot touch it at all. ★ The argument "the Casimir effect was measured, therefore vacuum energy is real" stops right here — what was measured is a difference.

Exercises

  1. In §03, 3089 photons appeared and \(|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=1\) never moved. What is being protected?
    Show answer
    The commutation relation — that is, uncertainty. \(|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=1\) is the condition for a Bogoliubov transformation to be canonical, and classically it is \(\det M=1\) (phase-space area).
    ★ It is the same equation as Episode 36's "squeeze all you like, \(\Delta X_1\Delta X_2=1/4\) does not move."
    ★★ So dynamical-Casimir photons necessarily come in pairs (\(\alpha\) grows whenever \(\beta\) does) — you cannot grow one alone. ★ The correlated photon pairs seen in experiment are a direct consequence of this identity.
  2. §05 said heat is logarithmic time. Does anything look hot if you measure time logarithmically?
    Show answer
    No — logarithmic time alone is not enough. What is also needed is that the coordinate system sees only part of the vacuum. An accelerating observer has a horizon and cannot see beyond it.
    ★ Discard (trace out) the unseen part and what remains is a mixed state — that is why it looks thermal. Logarithmic time appears as the form of the coordinate change that creates the horizon.
    ★★ So using logarithmic time in Episode 20's \(c\cdot t=\)const system does not by itself produce heat. ★ What is required is an unseen region — entropy appears in proportion to the information thrown away (Episode 21).
  3. §02's Casimir pressure went as \(a^{-4}\). Where does that exponent come from?
    Show answer
    Pure dimensional analysis. The only ingredients are \(\hbar,c,a\). Pressure has dimensions [energy]/[length]³ and \(\hbar c\) is [energy]×[length], so \(P\propto\hbar c/a^4\) — everything but the coefficient is one line.
    ★ On Episode 43's single table of orders, \(a^{-4}\) is a consequence of three dimensions. In \(d\) dimensions \(P\propto\hbar c/a^{d+1}\) — and §01's one-dimensional case gave \(F\propto1/a^2\), consistent.
    ★★ Only the coefficient (\(\pi^2/240\)) comes from "how the waves are counted" — the exponent is set by dimension, the coefficient by mode counting. Exactly the division of labour of Episode 30.

Summary — heat is time measured logarithmically

The sum of zero-point energies diverges, but the divergent term \(1/\delta^2\) is proportional to volume, so taking the difference between having walls and not removes it — leaving \(-1/12\) (numerically \(-0.0833333\)). The right reading is not "we subtracted an infinity" but "we took a difference." In one dimension, \(E=-\pi\hbar c/(24a)\), and the force is attractive.

For the 3D electromagnetic field, \(P=-\pi^2\hbar c/(240a^4)\) — 1.28 atmospheres at 10 nm. Being \(a^{-4}\), one decade closer is 10000 times more.

Shake a boundary and photons come out of nothing. \(n=0\) at \(h=0\); \(n=0.101\) at \(h=0.4\); \(n=3089\) after 60 periods.

★★★ And 3089 photons later, \(|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2=\det M=1\) has not moved —
the particle-number version of Episode 36's "\(\det M=1\) preserves uncertainty."

Accelerate and the vacuum looks warm (\(T=\hbar a/2\pi ck_B\)). 1 K needs \(2.5\times10^{20}\) m/s² — out of everyday reach.
