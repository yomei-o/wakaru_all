01★ The digital fractional derivative ── Grünwald–Letnikov

Just a binomial expansion $$(1-z^{-1})^\alpha=\sum_k w_k\,z^{-k},\qquad w_k=(-1)^k\binom{\alpha}{k}$$

By recursion, \(w_0=1,\ w_k=w_{k-1}\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha+1}{k}\right)\). The \(w_k\) are the filter's taps

The heart of §01

★★ Only when \(\alpha\) is a non-negative integer do the taps terminate (FIR).

★ \(\alpha=-1\) is all ones ── an accumulator: whatever goes in stays forever.
★ \(\alpha=0\) passes straight through (\(\delta\)). ★ \(\alpha=1\) is the difference \([1,-1]\), two taps.
★ \(\alpha=\pm1/2\) never ends.

04In the frequency domain ── which discretization preserves the order?

There is more than one way to replace \(s\) by \(z\). Put them on the unit circle \(z=e^{i\theta}\) (\(\theta=\omega T\)) and look at the phase.

Backward difference $$1-e^{-i\theta}=2\sin\frac\theta2\cdot e^{i(\pi/2-\theta/2)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{phase}=\alpha\left(90^\circ-\frac\theta2\right)$$

Exercises

  1. You want a half-order filter in an audio device. Backward difference or bilinear?
    Show the answer
    Bilinear. The backward difference gives a phase \(\alpha(90^\circ-\theta/2)\), so past 1 % of Nyquist it is already off by more than a degree. At the top of the audible band (20 kHz against a 24 kHz Nyquist, i.e. 0.83) the backward-difference phase has essentially collapsed.
    ★ Bilinear keeps the phase at exactly \(90\alpha^\circ\) at every frequency because \(\tan(\theta/2)\) is purely imaginary. The price is frequency warping, \(\omega\to(2/T)\tan(\omega T/2)\) (pre-warping is the standard fix).
    ★ Episode 1's "the order is the phase" becomes a design criterion.
  2. The \(\alpha=-1/2\) taps are all positive; the \(\alpha=+1/2\) taps are negative from the second onward. Why?
    Show the answer
    Because the sign of the asymptote \(w_k\approx k^{-\alpha-1}/\Gamma(-\alpha)\) is set by \(\Gamma(-\alpha)\). At \(\alpha=-1/2\), \(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi>0\); at \(\alpha=+1/2\), \(\Gamma(-1/2)=-2\sqrt\pi<0\).
    ★ In meaning ── the integrating side (\(\alpha<0\)) accumulates the past, so every tap is positive; the differentiating side (\(\alpha>0\)) subtracts it, so they go negative.
    ★ The extreme case is \(\alpha=-1\) (pure integration), all ones ── exactly Episode 30's \(d=1\), "a step that never leaves".
  3. If "Huygens holds ＝ FIR", what would creatures in a non-FIR dimension struggle with?
    Show the answer
    They could not separate moments in time, because the past keeps mixing in. In \(d=2\) the reverberation piles up as \(\sqrt n\) (§06), so the background rises the more you speak.
    ★ To hear "when" something sounded, the impulse response must be finite ── a different constraint from Episode 9's bandwidth theorem (\(\Delta t\Delta\omega\ge1/2\)). However wide your bandwidth, an IIR medium muddies timing.
    ★ Only 3 dimensions has \(\alpha=0\) (one tap), so conversation is a privilege of three dimensions (Episode 30).

Summary　The order becomes visible as a number of taps

We moved the stage from the \(s\) plane to the \(z\) plane. Expanding \(D^\alpha=(1-z^{-1})^\alpha\) binomially gives the Grünwald–Letnikov fractional derivative, and the coefficients \(w_k\) are the filter's taps.

★ Only for non-negative integer \(\alpha\) do the taps terminate (FIR): 1 tap at \(\alpha=0\), \([1,-1]\) at \(\alpha=1\), \([1,-2,1]\) at \(\alpha=2\). Otherwise they continue forever, decaying as \(w_k\approx k^{-\alpha-1}/\Gamma(-\alpha)\) (agreeing to 6 digits out to \(k=10^5\)). ★ The same shape as Episode 26's \(g(\tau)\propto\tau^{\alpha-1}\) ── continuous versus discrete is the only difference.

We checked it too ── convolving the \(D^{1/2}\) taps with themselves gives exactly \([1,-1]\). An infinite filter applied twice ends after two taps. The half order really is the square root of the first.

The order also decides how to discretize: the bilinear (Tustin) transform holds the phase at exactly \(90\alpha^\circ\) at every frequency (because \(\tan(\theta/2)\) is purely imaginary), while the backward difference is already off by a degree past 1 % of Nyquist even at first order. ★ Episode 1's "the order is the phase" became a design criterion.

★★★ And last episode's "dimension" was exactly the same count.
Odd dimensions ＝ integer order ＝ FIR (\((d-1)/2\) taps); even dimensions ＝ half-integer ＝ IIR.
The dimension of space can be counted as a filter's tap count.

\(d=1\) is the all-ones accumulator (\(\theta(s)\)), \(d=3\) the single-tap pass-through (\(\delta\)), \(d=5\) the two-tap difference (\(\delta'\)) ── matching Episode 30's table line for line. And the \(d=2\) reverberation piles up as \(\sqrt n\), with the ratio converging to \(2/\sqrt\pi=1.1284\).

Finally, the problem that only appears digitally has the same shape ── an integer delay is one tap; a half-sample delay is an infinite tail decaying only as \(1/k\). In the order, in the delay, and in the dimension: leave the integers and a tail grows.

On the numbers here: the GL coefficients and their asymptotics, the self-convolution of the half order, the phase of each discretization, the step responses, and the fractional-delay sinc coefficients were all computed for this article. The Grünwald–Letnikov definition, the coefficient asymptotics \(w_k\sim k^{-\alpha-1}/\Gamma(-\alpha)\), the fact that the bilinear transform maps the imaginary axis onto the unit circle, and that the ideal fractional delay is a sinc are all well-known results and not discoveries of this article ── what this article did was line them up against Episode 30's "dimension = order" table row by row and make the correspondence "odd dimensions = FIR, even dimensions = IIR" explicit. That correspondence means the Green's functions have the same shape; it is not a claim that the \(d\)-dimensional wave equation is literally a GL filter (one is an order in time, the other a discrete-time operator). The phase-error table in §04 is for the first-order backward difference; higher-order discretizations do better.
