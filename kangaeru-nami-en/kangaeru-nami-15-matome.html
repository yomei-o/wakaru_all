§02–03 ── two dividing lines

Line 1 (Episode 7): hits are dimensionless, misses are dimensionful.
\(\alpha\) and \(\mu=m_p/m_e\) really are integrals and really run. \(c\), \(\hbar\), \(k_B\) turn into unit conventions and differentiate to zero by definition.

★ Line 2 (Episode 14): the order of a law and the exponent of a statistic are different things.
Warburg's \(\alpha=-1/2\) is an equation that is genuinely half-order. Turbulence's \(-5/3\) comes from an integer-order equation, after statistics.
── The same "fraction", entirely different provenance.

04Limits of the framework itself

Precisely because it worked, it is worth writing down where it breaks.

①
It assumes stationarityFourier is the tool for things that oscillate the same way forever. A one-off jump spreads thinly over all frequencies and escapes (Episode 4)
②
It assumes linearitywaves superpose, but general relativity is non-linear. In strong fields nothing can be written as a "filter"
③
\(\omega=0\) is out of reachin principle, because it is the value itself (Episode 3)
④
Small effects are invisible★ we failed at this in Episode 13. The log-periodic tail is crushed by \(e^{-\beta\pi}\), \(10^{-6}\) at \(\beta=3\) ── smaller than the numerical artefact
Putting a number on ② ── how linear is it? $$\frac{GM}{Rc^2}:\quad \text{Earth's surface}\ 7\times10^{-10},\quad \text{solar surface}\ 2\times10^{-6},\quad \text{neutron star}\ 0.2,\quad \text{horizon}\ 0.5$$

On Earth it is linear to 10 digits ── which is why wave language works so well

§05 ── a narrow corner

Physics lives in the narrow rectangle \(\alpha\in[-1/2,\,2]\), \(\beta\approx0\).

Lower bound \(-1/2\): below it no causal response can be built (the diffusion boundary).
Upper bound 2: above it the Ostrogradsky instability sets in (and indeed order-3 radiation reaction produces pre-acceleration).
Imaginary part 0: \(\beta\neq0\) requires a special balance such as \(1/r^2\).

★ "Why does physics live in this rectangle?" ── that is the question this series arrived at.

§06 ── two new doors

Parts IV–VI added two questions, both about the order.

① Where the bounds \(-1/2\) and \(2\) come from. At present they are explained separately (the diffusion boundary; Ostrogradsky). If one principle gave both, that would be close to an answer to "why is physics shaped this way".

② What turns the imaginary part on. The only known case is a critical \(1/r^2\). Are there others, or is \(1/r^2\) essentially unique? ── if Episode 13's hypothesis is right (that \(1/r^2\) is the only potential with the same \(r\)-dependence as the kinetic energy), then the latter.

※ We have solved neither. Putting them into the form of questions is the result.

The final answer

"Are all physical constants integrals of something?" ──
Half right, and the right half sits at the centre of particle physics.

The right half: couplings are literally the integral of a beta function. \(1/\alpha\) runs from 137.036 to 128.940.
The wrong half: dimensionful constants cannot be integrated into. They turn into unit conventions.

★ And the real answer lay outside the question ──
what this series found was not "what constants are" but "the axis of order".

Exercises

  1. Name this series' biggest hit and biggest miss.
    Show answer
    Hit: mass = cutoff frequency (Episode 6). The Yukawa potential, \(E=mc^2\) and the relativistic energy relation all came out of one dispersion relation.
    Miss: mass = the integral of the universe's gravity (Episode 7). A beautiful 1/2 appeared, but it was an identity, and the testable version is excluded at \(10^{-5}\).
    What is striking is that the same "mass" succeeded completely one way and failed completely the other. The difference: seen as a wave, or seen as an integral.
    ★ And the biggest surprise was Episode 13 ── making the order imaginary moved us from time translation to scale transformation, an entirely different symmetry.
  2. There are two kinds of "fractional order". Distinguish them.
    Show answer
    ① The law itself is fractional-order: the Warburg impedance \((i\omega)^{-1/2}\), the springpot of viscoelasticity. A fractional operator literally appears in the equation.
    ② The exponent of a statistic is fractional: turbulence's \(-5/3\). The governing equation (Navier–Stokes) stays integer-order; the fraction comes from dividing dimensions.
    ★ How to tell: ① has memory (an integral kernel), ② does not. ① is one system's response; ② is the statistical average over many degrees of freedom.
    Episode 14 is where we noticed we had been conflating them.
  3. Where should this view be taken next?
    Show answer
    Two directions.
    A: toward dimensionless quantities (Episode 8's answer). Of the Standard Model's twenty-odd dimensionless parameters, not one has been derived as "the integral of" anything. Couplings run, but their initial values remain inputs.
    B: toward the boundaries of the order (this episode's answer). Why \(\alpha\in[-1/2,\,2]\)? The lower bound is causality, the upper stability ── but both look as though they come from time running one way only.
    ★ B has the narrower reach but a sharper question, which makes it easier to start on ── that is the impression after fifteen episodes.

Summary　The framework stood, and the protagonist changed

It began with "differentiating audio makes it brighter". Measured: \(\pm6.02\) dB/oct, phase \(\pm90°\), nine digits ── the foundation was exact.

Making the order continuous, \(\alpha=-1/2\) turned out to be real, and at \(\omega=0\) the \(1/\omega\) diverges ── the true identity of the constant of integration, where the physical constants live. In time they are flat, but change the axis to \(\ln E\) and \(1/\alpha\) runs from 137.036 to 128.940 ── "constants are integrals" is literally true there.

Seen as a wave, mass is a cutoff frequency. Seen as an integral of gravity, the beautiful 1/2 is an identity and the testable form is excluded. Hits are dimensionless, misses are dimensionful.

And from Part IV the protagonist changed ── causality is analyticity, uncertainty is the bandwidth theorem, the arrow of time is the parity of the order, heat does not travel because the orders are mismatched, the sky is blue because a second derivative was squared, and turbulence's \(-5/3\) is a determinant. Then in Episode 13 the order widened into the imaginary direction and became the generator of scale transformations.

★ Physics lives in the narrow rectangle \(\alpha\in[-1/2,\,2]\), \(\beta\approx0\).
"Why this rectangle?" ── that is the wall this series reached.

A pause here (Episodes 1–15) Doors closed: mass = integral of gravity (refuted), light speed = integral of information (a matter of definition), time variation of the constants (bounds only), turbulence = evidence of fractional order (a different animal).
Doors still open: the origin of \(1/f\), fractional order versus coarse-graining, the value of \(\alpha\), the \(10^{4}\sim10^{10}\) yr observational gap, why the bounds on the order are what they are, and whether \(\beta\neq0\) needs \(1/r^2\).
